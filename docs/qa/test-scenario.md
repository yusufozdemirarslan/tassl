# Kavena Loop: Choosing a Revenue Model for an AI Reflection Companion

Kavena Loop offers a voice-based AI reflection app. People record a few minutes about a workday, a difficult conversation, a goal, or an idea. The app returns a short reflection and saves a private timeline they can revisit. Its basic experience is free, and the company is deciding how to earn revenue without undermining user trust.

## The decision

In September 2026, Kavena Loop's leadership team was preparing for a board meeting. The app had drawn substantial interest, but its free product had not produced a durable business model. The board wanted one primary direction for the next 90 days and a credible explanation of what management would learn before committing more capital. A subscription launch could produce revenue soon, but nobody knew whether users would pay and renew. Employers expressed interest in a version for their staff, but buying cycles were slow and privacy expectations conflicted with what at least one buyer requested. A structured wellbeing program might eventually create a differentiated product, but it would require evidence and investment the company did not yet have. Management could also focus on improving retention before asking customers to pay. That choice might strengthen every later revenue option, but it would use scarce time and cash.

Kavena Loop's chief executive, Mara Solis, put the board's challenge plainly: "We can make a persuasive slide for any one of these paths. The harder question is what we actually know, what we are assuming, and what we are willing to put at risk." The board would authorize up to $120,000 for a focused 90-day test. It would not fund a collection of disconnected experiments. It wanted a recommendation with a first action, a defined customer population, a budget, a measurable outcome, and a decision checkpoint. Management could sequence later options, but it had to choose what would come first.

## What the product does

The app opens with a prompt such as "What is on your mind today?" A user can record for two to five minutes, receive a concise AI-generated reflection, and choose whether to save it. A private timeline lets the user revisit past entries. Some users treat it as a way to organize work decisions; others record after stressful days or use it to prepare for difficult conversations. Kavena Loop describes the app as a reflection tool. It does not claim to diagnose a condition, provide therapy, or treat symptoms.

The company has promised that private recordings will not be sold or used for targeted advertising. The basic recording and reflection experience is free. There are no ads. Users may delete entries, although the privacy lead wants the product to explain deletion and retention more clearly. The AI reflection is intended to summarize and prompt thought, not replace a person's judgment or a human professional. That distinction matters because some users speak about emotionally intense situations. The team has seen cases in which the assistant answered an urgent message with generic encouragement instead of making a clear route to human help visible.

Product lead Daniel Cho believes open reflection is the reason loyal users return. His team has resisted turning each session into a scripted lesson. At the same time, the open format can disappoint people who expect a structured journal, a coach, or a therapist. The company has to decide which promise to make to customers before deciding what to charge them for.

## What the usage numbers show

Since launch, Kavena Loop has accumulated 420,000 registered accounts. About 280,000 accounts completed at least one recording. In August 2026, 82,000 accounts were monthly active and 38,000 were weekly active. Monthly and weekly active users are subsets of registered accounts; they are not additional customers. An active account completed at least one recording in the relevant period. A registration may have occurred months earlier, and many registered users no longer open the app. The company has never offered an actual paid subscription, so none of these figures is a measured number of paying customers.

A recent cohort of 30,000 first-time recorders had 44% Day 1 retention, 18% Day 7 retention, and 7% Day 30 retention. For this measure, retention means that a person opened the app and completed another recording on the specified day. The cohort measure and August's active-user totals answer different questions. The cohort follows people who recently started; monthly active users count anyone who recorded during August. The dashboard cannot yet show how many weekly active users would buy specific premium features or how long buyers would remain subscribed.

The team interviewed 24 recently active users and 24 people who had stopped using the app. Several active interviewees said a short reflection gave them a reason to return, especially when they wanted to think through work or relationships without sharing the recording with others. Among the 24 who had stopped, 14 said the first experience promised more than it delivered or that they had expected a structured journaling tool rather than an open conversation. Interviewees volunteered to speak, so the counts are useful clues about product expectations, not representative estimates for all users.

Growth lead Aisha Nouri ran a randomized onboarding test with 30,000 new users split evenly between the old and redesigned introduction. The redesigned group had 22% Day 7 retention, compared with 18% in the old group. Day 30 retention was 8% in the redesigned group and 7% in the old group. The result suggests that setting expectations can improve early use, but the Day 30 difference is modest. It does not establish willingness to pay. Nouri wants to continue improving the first session, yet she acknowledges that retention work without a decision deadline could become a way to avoid testing a business model.

## The cash constraint

Kavena Loop holds $1.14 million in cash. It currently spends about $95,000 more than it earns each month. At that rate, before new investment or a change in revenue or spending, the cash balance represents roughly 12 months of runway. A 90-day wait at the current burn would use approximately $285,000. A full $120,000 test allowance, if spent in addition to the current burn, would reduce the remaining cash further. Finance lead Priya Raman cautions that a test is not free simply because the board approves it.

Raman is willing to fund learning, but she wants the team to specify a go, narrow, change, or stop rule before seeing the result. "If the outcome can support any recommendation we already wanted," she says, "we have paid for a nicer story, not a decision." She asks each proposal to state which denominator it uses, which rate is observed, and whether its economics include the costs necessary to win and retain customers. The board will look closely at any plan that treats a historical registration count as a current sales opportunity.

## Path one: consumer membership

The product team proposes keeping basic recording and reflection free while offering an $8-per-month membership. Proposed paid features include longer memory across entries, a weekly audio digest, and custom reflection routines. The team expects these additions to be useful to people who already record regularly. It has not established that they are valuable enough to sustain a paid subscription.

The finance team estimates $5.70 of monthly contribution per paying member after incremental AI processing, payment fees, and support. This estimate is before fixed operating costs and marketing. It is not an observed result because the tier has not been sold. At this estimated contribution, recovering $120,000 of one-time test spending from membership contribution alone would require about 21,053 paid member-months. The calculation does not include the company's existing monthly burn, acquisition costs, taxes, refunds, or the time needed to reach that volume. It is a scale check, not a business forecast.

A survey of 180 recently active users found that 42% said they would consider paying $8 for the proposed tier. Respondents were self-selected. They saw feature descriptions but did not enter payment information, choose between actual offers, or face a charge. The CEO thinks their enthusiasm merits a test; Raman says stated consideration is not purchase or renewal. A draft board slide goes further. It applies a 4% paying-member scenario to all 420,000 registered accounts, producing 16,800 hypothetical members and $134,400 in monthly gross subscription revenue at $8 each. The arithmetic is correct if both assumptions hold. The slide provides no observed paid conversion rate, however, and the historical registration base is not the same as a reachable group of active users. Even the $134,400 figure is gross revenue, not monthly contribution or cash profit.

Nouri proposes a narrower 90-day offer test among recently weekly active users. The team could randomly invite an eligible group to a real purchase page at $8, record purchase rather than survey intent, and follow buyers through at least one renewal opportunity. It could compare usage among invited and uninvited free users to see whether the offer makes the basic experience feel degraded. It would also learn which premium feature creates value. But a narrow cohort test may reveal a lower purchase rate than the board's slide suggests. If only the most engaged users will pay, the company must avoid applying their rate to inactive accounts.

Solis will support a paid tier only if the free core remains useful. She worries that withholding a person's past reflections behind a paywall could make users feel their own history had been taken from them. She also wants the company to avoid features designed to maximize time spent in conversation when longer use may reflect distress or dependence rather than customer value. The membership path is attractive for speed and direct customer learning. Its central uncertainties are actual purchase, renewal, incremental cost, and the effect of monetization on trust.

## Path two: an organizational offer

Two employers, each with approximately 800 eligible staff, have signed nonbinding letters expressing interest in a pilot at a proposed price of $4 per eligible employee per month. Neither has signed a paid contract. If both bought for all 1,600 eligible staff at the proposed price, the nominal monthly bill would be $6,400 before implementation, support, procurement, and other costs. That is a conditional illustration, not contracted revenue. Sales lead Owen Bell estimates that procurement may take four to six months. A buyer may also want proof that staff use the app before agreeing to pay.

Bell sees a path to distribution through employers that could spare Kavena Loop some individual customer-acquisition expense. The proposed offer would allow staff to use the reflection app voluntarily while an employer pays for access. An employer asked whether managers could see which employees recorded after difficult shifts. Privacy lead Leila Haddad refused. She will not provide identifiable recordings, individual usage histories, or inferred emotional states to an employer. Her alternative is aggregate participation reporting with minimum group sizes and no transcript access. She also wants plain notice to employees about what the organization can and cannot see.

Bell recognizes that these safeguards might disappoint some buyers. He also argues that employees will not use a sensitive reflection product if they suspect their manager can inspect it. A privacy-bounded pilot therefore has two customers whose interests must both be addressed: the organization paying for access and the employee deciding whether to trust the tool. A contract can be commercially promising while the product itself fails to earn employee adoption. Conversely, employee interest does not establish that procurement will succeed.

The team has not costed a full enterprise rollout. A pilot would need contract language, consent and access rules, reporting limits, an implementation plan, support, and a way to measure voluntary use without exposing individual behavior. Kavena Loop could allocate the 90-day test allowance to negotiating a narrowly defined pilot and demonstrating that employees will choose to use it under real privacy terms. The risk is that the 90 days end with another expression of interest rather than a paid commitment. The board must decide how much of the runway to spend on a path with a longer buying cycle.

## Path three: a structured wellbeing program

Clinical advisor Dr. Elena Voss believes Kavena Loop could develop more structured exercises with clearer boundaries, reviewed language, and measured outcomes. She estimates that an initial protocol, safety evaluation, and supervised pilot would cost about $250,000 over six months. That exceeds the board's immediate $120,000 allowance and requires a separate financing decision. There is no evidence that the current app reduces symptoms or delivers a clinical benefit. Voss opposes calling it a therapist or treating user testimonials as evidence of efficacy.

Voss proposes a staged first step instead of pretending the whole six-month program can fit inside the 90-day budget. The first stage would define what the product may and may not claim, review high-risk interactions, improve routes to human help, and assess whether a structured exercise can be tested safely. It would not establish clinical effectiveness. A later supervised pilot would require outcome measures, qualified oversight, and funding beyond the present allowance.

Cho worries that too much scripting could remove the open reflection that users value. Solis sees a possible way to serve people better but is unwilling to market treatment before evidence exists. Raman asks whether the company could afford a longer program while still searching for revenue. The board must weigh a potentially stronger long-term position against a higher upfront cost, a slower route to revenue, and the risk of making claims the evidence does not support.

## Path four: retention before monetization

Nouri argues that the company could use the 90 days to improve onboarding, clarify the product promise, and test whether people return for meaningful reasons. The randomized introduction test gives this path some support. The company could compare a more structured first reflection with the current open prompt, test whether users understand what their entries become, and measure repeat recordings rather than downloads or registrations. A higher Day 30 retention rate would enlarge the active population available for any later paid offer.

Raman accepts the logic but asks when it ends. A retention-first plan needs an explicit threshold, a spending cap, and a date for a commercial decision. Otherwise, the company may spend another quarter improving the free app without testing purchase, contracts, or a product people will fund. Nouri says a strong version of her plan would include a defined revenue decision at the end of 90 days, not an indefinite delay. The board must decide whether improving the underlying experience is the best first use of cash or whether a paid offer should be tested while the product team continues modest onboarding work.

## A boundary every option must respect

The safety team reviewed nine flagged urgent messages. Three were handled later than the company's internal response target, and some received generic encouragement instead of a clear route to human help. Nine messages are too few to estimate the population-wide failure rate, but the review shows a concrete problem to fix. Haddad wants any 90-day plan to include a safer handoff, a review of whether the assistant stays within its stated reflection role, and a way to learn from failures without exposing private recordings unnecessarily.

Privacy and safety are not side projects that can be addressed after revenue appears. Membership could create incentives to prolong conversations. An organizational offer could expose sensitive information or change what staff are willing to record. A structured wellbeing program could lead users to rely on the app beyond its evidence and capabilities. Retention experiments could reward repeat use without asking whether the use was helpful. Kavena Loop needs measures of user value and clear limits on data access, claims, and escalation for whichever path it chooses.

## Evidence packet: product and customer notes, 3 September 2026

Cho's product memo describes three jobs users appear to bring to the app. Some want to organize a decision after a meeting or conversation. Some want a record of their thoughts over time. Others arrive during a stressful period and look for immediate reassurance. The memo warns that the same interface may appear to satisfy each job while delivering different value. A concise AI reflection can help one user see a tradeoff and leave another feeling that the app merely rephrased what they said. A saved timeline is useful only if people return and can find meaning in older entries. A guided routine may help a new user start but make a loyal user feel constrained.

The product team has not mapped these jobs to a reliable market-size estimate. Its 24 interviews with recently active users were recruited from people willing to speak to the company; the 24 interviews with users who left were recruited the same way. A project manager said she recorded after a tense meeting to separate the facts from her interpretation. A shift supervisor said she would stop using the app if her employer could see the days on which she recorded. A former user said an AI response sounded polished but did not help him make a better decision. Another former user expected a journal with prompts and felt unsure what to say to an open microphone. These comments illuminate possible sources of value and disappointment. They do not prove the prevalence of any segment or what that segment will pay.

Cho proposes testing one clear product promise rather than describing the app as a coach, journal, mental-health service, and work benefit at once. A membership offer centered on "remember and reflect on your own history" might appeal to regular users. A guided-routine offer might help people who struggle with an open prompt. Neither should remove access to basic recording. Solis is particularly concerned that charging for access to past entries would feel as if the company had taken ownership of a user's thoughts. She wants cancellation terms to be legible before anyone buys.

The analytics team can identify repeat recording, but it cannot yet measure whether a reflection helped a decision, supported a useful habit, or prolonged an unhelpful session. The team could ask users whether they found a session useful, but self-reported usefulness would still require careful interpretation. Repeated use may indicate value or may reflect an unresolved problem. Any test that simply rewards more minutes in conversation could pull the product away from its stated purpose.

## Evidence packet: employer conversations, 9 September 2026

Bell's sales note records two nonbinding letters from employers with about 800 eligible staff each. Both express interest in a pilot at a proposed $4 per eligible employee per month; neither is a purchase order or a contract. Bell expects procurement to take four to six months. The buyers could request legal review, a security assessment, and proof that staff voluntarily use the benefit. A 90-day test might produce a signed pilot, but it might also end with another conversation and no billable seats.

One buyer wants a low-friction employee benefit and asks for group-level participation reporting. Another asks whether a manager could see which employees recorded after difficult shifts. Haddad refuses individual recording dates, transcripts, inferred emotional states, and employee-level use histories. She proposes aggregate reporting with minimum group sizes and suppressed results for small teams. She wants the contract to state that individual content will not be available to the employer and wants a plain-language employee notice that says the same thing.

Bell worries that strict limits could make some buyers less interested. He also believes employees will withhold honest reflections if they suspect their managers are watching. An employer contract and employee trust are separate tests. The paying organization may value an available benefit, while the person who records may judge its privacy promise. A pilot could fail either side. A high seat count on a contract is not proof of voluntary use. Strong voluntary use is not proof that the buyer will pay after procurement.

Bell suggests a narrow pilot with one employer, a defined launch group, an employer-paid contract, and explicit privacy terms. The team would measure aggregate activation and repeat recording without revealing individuals. It would gather anonymous feedback on whether employees understood what the employer could see. Bell asks for a decision rule: if the employer insists on surveillance-like features, should Kavena Loop walk away even if that delays revenue? Haddad says yes. Solis says the board should treat such a refusal as evidence about which organizational customers fit the product rather than a failure of sales execution.

## Evidence packet: clinical and safety review, 11 September 2026

Voss's note separates three questions. Can the assistant consistently stay within a reflection role and respond appropriately when someone needs human help? Can a structured exercise be understood and used without making misleading promises? Does the exercise produce a measurable benefit compared with a suitable alternative? The first two can be investigated in a bounded first stage. The third requires a properly designed supervised evaluation and cannot be inferred from testimonials, repeated use, or a polished demo.

The safety team reviewed nine flagged urgent messages. Three were handled later than the company's internal response target. In some reviewed messages, the assistant offered generic encouragement instead of a clear route to human support. Nine messages are far too few to estimate a population-wide failure rate, but the cases show that the escalation design needs work. Haddad wants the team to define what counts as urgent, what response is expected, how delays are found, who reviews a private recording, and how the company can learn from a failure without turning sensitive content into a broad analytics feed.

Voss warns against describing the current product as a therapist. A clinician helping to write a reflection routine does not prove a clinical outcome. A "wellbeing" label may sound like general lifestyle support to one buyer and a treatment promise to another. The assistant's words, product copy, customer contract, and evidence must align. If Kavena Loop pursues a structured program, the first 90 days should define claim boundaries and safety procedures, not assert effectiveness.

Solis asks whether safety work should precede every revenue test. Haddad says a baseline handoff improvement and clear claim limits must be part of any test, but the company can still learn about willingness to pay or organizational trust while doing that work. Raman asks what portion of the $120,000 allowance the baseline safety improvement would consume and whether management will report it separately from customer acquisition or clinical research. The question forces the leaders to distinguish a minimum product obligation from a revenue-model experiment.

## Evidence packet: four proposed 90-day plans, 12 September 2026

The membership plan would show a real $8 offer to a randomly selected group of recently weekly active users. It would keep basic recording free, test one paid benefit at a time, count completed purchases among everyone assigned to see the offer, and observe at least the first renewal. It would measure contribution using actual incremental servicing and payment costs and check whether the offer harms free-user trust or use. Its fastest possible positive signal is payment. Its unresolved question after 90 days may be long-term retention beyond the first renewal.

The organizational plan would try to move one interested employer from a nonbinding letter to a paid, privacy-bounded pilot. It would test whether a buyer accepts aggregate-only reporting and whether staff understand and voluntarily use the benefit. Its strongest 90-day signal is a signed contract with enforceable data limits and a launch plan. Its weakness is that procurement could last longer than the test and the economics of a scaled account remain unknown.

The retention-first plan would clarify the opening product promise, test an open versus guided first experience, and track repeat recording in a defined cohort. It would set a Day 30 improvement target and a date for a paid-offer or contract decision. Its advantage is improving the active population from which any revenue model would draw. Its weakness is that better use of a free app is not evidence that anyone will fund it.

The structured-program plan would spend a bounded first stage on reviewed exercises, safety handoff, explicit claim limits, and a supervised pilot design. It would ask the board for a separate financing decision before proceeding toward the estimated $250,000 six-month program. Its advantage is a clearer long-term product boundary. Its weakness is that it may use scarce cash without testing a near-term buyer or producing evidence of clinical benefit.

These plans are alternatives, not four simultaneous workstreams. The board has not approved a detailed budget for any one of them. Management must name its primary test, propose how it will spend no more than $120,000, and describe what evidence will trigger the next decision. A small supporting action may be included if it is necessary to run the primary test safely. It cannot make the proposal a disguised attempt to pursue every option.

## The board meeting

The executives bring different views to the meeting. Solis favors a direct-to-consumer reflection product with a useful free core. Raman doubts that the large registration total can be turned into recurring revenue without a real purchase test. Bell sees distribution through organizations but no signed contracts and difficult privacy negotiations. Voss sees potential for structured support, not a clinical product ready to sell. Nouri believes the onboarding result justifies another focused test, but not a broad launch. Cho wants to preserve the product experience that engaged users already value. Haddad insists that the company's promises about recordings and human help must survive the business-model decision.

No one can make all the uncertainty disappear in 90 days. The board wants a decision that is proportionate to the evidence. It will consider a consumer membership test, a privacy-bounded organizational pilot, a bounded retention-first test, or a first-stage structured-program evaluation. It will also consider a sequence, provided management names one primary action now and does not use the sequence to postpone the choice.

You are advising the leadership team before the board vote. Recommend one primary path for the next 90 days. State how you would use no more than $120,000 of test spending, what customer group you would target, and which denominator should anchor any revenue expectation. Separate observed facts from estimates and planning scenarios. Explain the strongest alternative to your choice and the tradeoff you are accepting. Specify what result would cause you to continue, expand, narrow, change direction, or stop at the checkpoint. Finally, set the privacy, product-claim, and urgent-distress boundaries that your plan must satisfy even if its revenue indicators improve. The board will accept a conditional recommendation. It will not accept a plan that makes every outcome sound like success.

## Appendix: how to read the evidence packet

The following dated materials were circulated to Kavena Loop's board before the September meeting. They do not replace the decision at the end of the case. They show how different leaders interpreted the same uncertain product and how a 90-day test might be designed. A proposed target, budget, or threshold is a planning choice, not an observed outcome. When figures differ in purpose, the distinction matters. Historical registrations are cumulative; recent active accounts are counted by use in a time window; a cohort retention rate follows first-time recorders; a survey answer is stated intention; a purchase is a completed payment; and a renewal is another payment after the first subscription period. The board expects management to use the right measure for the claim being made.

### Exhibit A — Product experience walkthrough, 1 September 2026

A new Kavena Loop user sees a short explanation: "Speak for a few minutes. Get a reflection that helps you notice what matters. Keep your entries private." The screen then asks what is on their mind. There is no required daily streak and no diagnostic questionnaire. After recording, the app returns a short paragraph that identifies themes and offers a question the person may consider. The user can save the entry in a private timeline, discard it, or record again. The reflection is meant to help someone organize thought, not to tell them what choice to make. The company believes this discretion is part of the product's appeal.

The walkthrough identifies three moments when expectations can diverge. Before recording, a user may think the app will act as a guided journal or personal coach because it uses conversational AI. During recording, the user may discuss an emotionally intense situation that a generic reflection is poorly suited to handle. After receiving an elegant response, the user may mistake fluent wording for deeper understanding than the system actually has. Each moment has a product and commercial implication. A clearer introduction may improve retention. Better boundaries may protect trust. A paid tier that promises deeper insight without demonstrable difference may create disappointment even if some people purchase it once.

The team has drafted an alternative first-session introduction that asks the user to choose between an open recording and three simple prompts: "What happened?", "What am I assuming?", and "What might I do next?" The prompts are not a treatment protocol. Cho wants to test whether they help people understand what the product does, especially those who feel lost when facing an open microphone. He worries that making the prompts mandatory could reduce the freedom loyal users value. Nouri favors an optional choice and a measured comparison with the existing introduction.

The proposed membership features change the same journey. Longer memory would connect themes across the user's past entries. A weekly digest would make it easier to revisit those themes without rereading a timeline. Custom routines would add a chosen structure. Product design has not determined how much additional AI processing each feature requires in actual use. Finance has modeled an average incremental cost, but a heavy member who generates frequent long digests might be more expensive to serve than a light member. Cho asks for an experiment that records serving costs by feature and does not hide a negative margin among heavy users inside a single average.

Cancellation also creates a trust test. The team agrees that a member who cancels should still be able to access and delete their own saved entries. Premium generation could stop at the end of the paid period, but an entry created while paying should not disappear. A purchase page that fails to explain this could raise conversion in the short term and damage credibility later. Solis wants purchase, cancellation, and data-access language reviewed together. She argues that a recurring product depends on users believing the company will keep its promises after the first payment.

### Exhibit B — User interview synthesis, 2 September 2026

The research team spoke with 24 recently active users and 24 former users recruited through voluntary invitations. It did not draw a random sample. The interview guide asked what led someone to record, what the returned reflection changed, whether they revisited a saved entry, and what they expected the app to do. Researchers did not ask the volunteers to make a real purchase, and they did not score mental-health outcomes. Their notes identify patterns worth testing rather than rates that can be applied to the whole population.

Active participants described private thought organization as the most reliable use. One project manager recorded after client meetings to sort decisions from emotions. A graduate student used the timeline to see how her view of a job offer changed over three weeks. A night-shift employee valued that no manager saw the recording. Other active users liked the voice format because speaking was faster than typing. A few said the app felt most useful when its reflection pointed to a question they had avoided. The company has no validated measure of how often that happens.

Former users gave several reasons for leaving. Fourteen of the 24 said the first experience had promised more than it delivered or had led them to expect a structured journaling tool. Some had assumed the app would remember and reason across all previous entries even though the free reflection was based mainly on the current recording. Others felt they were speaking into a polished paraphrase rather than getting a useful question. A few disliked recording sensitive material at all. The voluntary interview method cannot show which explanation is most common among all churned users. It does tell the team that a broader membership promise should be specific about what changes from free to paid.

Researchers also noted a distinction between curiosity and routine. Someone may register because a friend shared an example or because the AI response looks novel. That person may not have a recurring reason to record. A regular user may return because the app fits a weekly habit, but still prefer free access and decline an $8 charge. Another person may pay for longer memory and then cancel after discovering that the digest adds little value. The synthesis ends with questions for each path. For membership: which feature causes a person to pay, and does that person renew after using it? For employers: do employees understand that their recordings are private from managers, and do they use the benefit voluntarily? ### Exhibit C — Funnel definitions and a disputed forecast, 4 September 2026

The growth dashboard is easiest to misuse when all of its figures appear on a single slide. It lists 420,000 cumulative registered accounts, 280,000 accounts with at least one completed recording, 82,000 monthly active accounts in August, and 38,000 weekly active accounts in a recent August week. The four values are nested or overlapping measures, not separate pools to add. The first two describe what happened since launch. The latter two describe recent behavior. A current offer can reach only people who are still reachable through the product or a permissioned channel. The company has not measured that reachable population for the proposed paid tier.

The first-time-recorder cohort is another kind of evidence. Thirty thousand recent first-time recorders were followed for return recording on specified days. Forty-four percent recorded again on Day 1, 18% on Day 7, and 7% on Day 30. Those percentages should not be multiplied into the 420,000 registration count to create a current active-user estimate. The cohort and cumulative registration pool differ in timing and selection. A paid buyer cohort would differ again because people who choose to pay may be unusually engaged.

The draft board forecast begins with a 4% scenario applied to cumulative registrations. It computes 16,800 members and then $134,400 gross monthly billings at $8. There is no error in multiplying the chosen numbers. The question is whether the company has grounds to use 4% and 420,000 as if they described the paying population next month. It has no real subscription offer, no observed purchase rate, and no evidence that all historical accounts can be reached. The slide also calls its gross billings "revenue potential" without showing the estimated $5.70 contribution per paid member or the time needed to reach the member count. A sound test forecast would begin with a named eligible population, such as weekly active accounts who receive a real invitation during the test. The first-renewal rate would use members who had a renewal opportunity as the denominator, not all registered accounts. Gross billings would count payments; contribution would deduct incremental costs; cash outcome would also account for test and operating spending. Even a carefully run 90-day test would require caution before its rate is applied to people outside the tested group.

The dashboard team proposes publishing a one-page "measure dictionary" beside future board slides. Each number would have a period, population, numerator, denominator, observation method, and caveat. ### Exhibit H — Retention experiment proposal, 12 September 2026

Nouri's retention proposal begins with a specific problem: many first-time users do not return after the novelty of speaking to an AI reflection tool wears off. The recent 30,000-person first-recorder cohort had 18% Day 7 and 7% Day 30 repeat recording. A redesigned introduction improved Day 7 retention to 22% and Day 30 to 8% in a randomized 30,000-user onboarding test. The result suggests that expectation-setting can matter. It does not show that one more revision will solve the longer-term habit problem.

The proposal would test two first-session choices. New users could start with the existing open prompt or see an optional guided route built around events, assumptions, and possible next actions. The team would not call the guided route treatment or therapy. It would make clear that the assistant returns a short reflection and that users choose what to keep. The experiment would assign new users randomly, capture whether they complete a first recording, and follow the same first-time-recorder cohort for repeat recording. The main outcome would be Day 30 return recording, with Day 7 and first-session completion as supporting measures. A registration count or a favorable reaction to the guided route would not by itself establish the objective.

Nouri also proposes a qualitative review of why people return. A person who records again because the product helps them prepare a decision may represent a more durable use than a person who returns to test another surprising AI response. That distinction cannot be fully resolved by usage analytics. A small interview set and an optional usefulness question could help interpret the metric, though neither should be extrapolated as a population outcome without a suitable sample. Cho asks the researchers to include people who were assigned to both versions and people who stopped using each, instead of interviewing only delighted returners.

The plan would consume design, implementation, measurement, and research time. Nouri suggests releasing spending in stages rather than committing the full $120,000 at once. She asks the board to set a Day 30 improvement threshold before the experiment starts and to specify what happens if the result remains close to 8%. A plausible response would be to stop expanding onboarding revisions and test a real paid offer among the active users the company already has. Another would be to narrow the product promise or reduce burn. The right threshold is a judgment about business value and cost, not a number already supplied by the case.

Raman's objection is timing. The company may leave the quarter with a better free product but still have no measured purchase rate or paid organizational contract. If the first result is promising, management can narrow or stage the next test; it cannot immediately claim the $134,400 monthly gross scenario as a forecast. Solis adds that a tier which charges for access to a user's own history is off the table. The team can sell new functionality, but the free relationship must remain credible.

Bell argues that a direct consumer subscription may leave the company paying to reach each buyer and fighting churn. An organization could distribute the app to hundreds of staff at once. Haddad asks whether Bell's prospects will sign without employee-level reports. He says one might; he does not know about the other. "Then the contract is the test," Haddad answers. "The price on the letter is not the test." Bell accepts that a buyer requesting individual emotional data may be a poor fit. He wants to learn whether a privacy-bounded buyer can still be found within the company's runway.

Voss objects to a debate that treats the app only as a revenue engine. She points to the nine urgent messages and the three late handoffs. Cho agrees the response needs improvement but asks whether that forces a six-month clinical program. Voss says it does not. A baseline handoff is necessary for any path; a structured program is a larger strategic choice that needs funding and evaluation. Raman asks Voss to define what the first stage would prove. Voss says it would prove the company can write and operate clearer boundaries, not that it has clinical efficacy or a buyer.

Nouri returns to retention. A Day 30 rate of 7% in the recent cohort suggests that the product promise is not yet working for many newcomers. She thinks a paid tier will be easier to judge once onboarding is clearer. Raman asks whether waiting a quarter will leave enough cash for the later paid test. Nouri concedes that a retention-first plan needs a stop rule and a date for a revenue decision. She refuses, however, to call a purchase experiment useful if users do not understand what they are buying.

Solis closes the discussion without declaring a winner. "Choose a first move that can change our mind," she says. "Put the best alternative beside it. Tell the board what you are giving up for the next 90 days and what boundary you will keep even if the numbers tempt us." The executives leave with different recommendations but agree on one rule: an attractive calculation cannot be promoted from scenario to evidence by repeating it more confidently.

### Exhibit Q — Measurement sheet for the next board update, 15 September 2026

The board asks every proposed plan to return with a measure sheet. For the consumer path, the sheet should name the eligible invitation population, how many were randomly assigned to the offer, how many were exposed, how many completed a payment, how many were eligible for first renewal, and how many renewed. It should show gross billings and estimated contribution separately, with actual serving cost checked against the $5.70 planning average. It should report free-user behavior and trust complaints, not only buyer behavior. If the test excludes inactive registered accounts, it should not project the observed rate onto them without an explicit argument.

For the organizational path, the sheet should distinguish letters, contracts signed, contracts started, billable eligible seats, employee activations, repeat recording, and aggregate-only reporting compliance. A buyer meeting is a milestone; a signed contract is a different milestone; a billable seat is another. Employee trust should be checked by asking whether staff understood what their organization could see. A team that reports only a large seat count could miss low voluntary use or a misleading privacy notice. Account contribution should be estimated after real implementation and support costs rather than from the $4 price alone.

For a retention-first path, the sheet should follow an assigned new-user cohort, not mix new users with August's broader active base. It should show first-recording completion, Day 7 repeat recording, Day 30 repeat recording, and whether the change in introductory language shifted people's understanding of the app. The earlier randomized onboarding result is a benchmark, not a promised outcome. A modest increase should trigger the precommitted commercial decision rather than an automatically extended free-product project. An increase in registrations is not a substitute for repeat use.

For a structured-program stage, the sheet should name which routine and claim limits were reviewed, what urgent-message scenarios were tested, how delayed handoffs were identified, and whether users understood the program's purpose. It should say which clinical questions remain untested and which later study would be needed. A user saying a routine felt helpful can be reported as a qualitative account; it cannot be entered as a clinical effect size. The financing request for a later supervised pilot must remain distinct from the board's current $120,000 allowance.

The measure sheet also carries a decision log. Management would write its primary recommendation, the three assumptions on which it rests, the expected result at each checkpoint, and the observation that would cause it to hold, narrow, revise, reverse, or stop. The purpose is to keep the company from writing a new story after the result arrives. The board understands that an unexpected result may deserve a changed plan. It wants the change explained against the original assumptions and the new evidence, not disguised as what management intended all along.

### Exhibit R — Chair's closing note

The chair reminds the executives that a pilot is a decision instrument, not a promise that the company has found its real model. The board can authorize a bounded test while remaining skeptical of a broad launch. It can approve a privacy-bounded contract effort while refusing employee surveillance. It can require safer handling of urgent messages while declining an unfunded clinical claim. It can invest in retention while demanding a date for commercial proof. Each test must say what it can and cannot show.

The chair asks the advisers to avoid one tempting shortcut. A polished AI summary of the packet may correctly multiply 420,000 by 4% and still misstate what the calculation proves. It may describe nonbinding employer letters as a pipeline and still leave out procurement and employee trust. It may call a reviewed routine safe without showing how urgent situations are handled. The executive who repeats those claims owns the recommendation. Before the vote, management should check the population, source, and status of any consequential figure it adopts.
