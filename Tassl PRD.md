# Tassl: Product Requirements Document

- **Tassl: Make the call.**
- **What it is: The AI decision simulator for higher education.**
- **What it promises: Practice before it matters.**
- **What it develops: Judgment students can carry into the real world.**

## Problem

Before AI, the work students produced served as evidence of what they understood and could do. AI has broken that relationship. Students can now produce expert-looking work before they can evaluate its assumptions, verify its claims, explain its reasoning, or take responsibility for the decision.

## Solution

Tassl gives students repeated practice making realistic, consequential decisions with AI before the consequences are real. It reveals how they think, what they trust, what they verify, and whether they can adapt and defend the final decision.

## 1. One-Paragraph Summary

Tassl is a practice environment where students in professional degree programs make consequential decisions with AI in the room and remain accountable for them. A student enters a realistic business situation with incomplete information, disagreeing stakeholders, an AI assistant that is useful but not uniformly reliable, and conditions that change partway through. They state the decision before AI enters, choose what to delegate, take a position on each consequential claim by accepting, verifying, challenging, rejecting, or escalating it, commit under a clock, respond when conditions shift, and defend the result without AI assistance. Tassl is designed to assess the decision process rather than the polish of the deliverable: framing, proportional reliance, verification, adaptation, and ownership. Tassl assesses a run the way a flight simulator supports an instructor's debrief: the run is recorded as instrument traces, the traces are plotted as graphs against the authored standard, draft bands on the seven dimensions (7.13) are read off the graphs, and the instructor debriefs from the same graphs and confirms or changes the bands. Tassl pronounces no verdict about the person; it shows what happened against an authored standard, and the course's points are the instructor's mapping of the bands (7.19). The immediate objective is the build: one working vertical slice of one Decision Run, accepted when the builder (the author of this document, who is building Tassl and teaches the pilot course) and the editor (the AI collaborator that edits this document and generates the scenario content) walk through it together with no students and no institutional approvals (Section 12, Definition of done). The build needs no institutional approvals because it creates no student data; content confirmation of the generated scenario by the builder as disciplinary authority still precedes the walkthrough. The stage after the build is a controlled GW pilot, whose objective is to test whether these behaviors can be captured validly, interpreted consistently by faculty, improved through repeated practice, and reviewed within an acceptable workload. The build proves the loop works; the pilot tests what it measures. Neither stage claims that judgment has already been measured or improved.

## 2. Problem and Context

A student presents a segmentation analysis, positioning statement, and media plan that would have taken an agency team a week five years ago. The professor asks why this segment and not the adjacent one, which assumption carries the recommendation, how the market-size figure was dated, what happens if acquisition cost is 40 percent higher. In 90 seconds the sophistication evaporates. The student did not cheat and worked for hours. They produced something whose internals they could not inspect.

The problem is not that AI lets students produce work beyond their unaided ability; spreadsheets and search engines always did that. It is that AI produces work beyond a student's evaluative competence while making it look authoritative. Fluency reads as validity, and the reader most likely to be fooled is the novice who commissioned it. Three forces sharpen the gap. AI systems are agreeable: push back without evidence and the assistant abandons a defensible position and congratulates the user, so confidence rather than evidence steers the analysis. AI systems present work they did not do: ask for a statistical test and you receive the method, plausible numbers, and an interpretation whether or not anything was computed. And a claim outside what a student has been taught cannot be corrected by that student.

Students can accumulate a portfolio while developing less of the capability it appears to represent, then encounter the shortfall when asked to explain or act on the work. Faculty interviews describe a collapse in the diagnostic value of polished submissions and identify oral defense as useful but difficult to repeat at scale. The exact size of the workload and courseware-cost problem varies by course and institution and will be measured in the pilot rather than treated as established. The institutional question is increasingly direct: what evidence of competence remains credible when sophisticated output is no longer scarce?

The opportunity has become both buildable and urgent. General-purpose assistants can already behave like competent but occasionally unreliable colleagues, and student use is widespread. Faculty discovery and the fellowship cohort show movement from blanket prohibition toward assessment designs that distinguish unaided knowledge from responsible AI-assisted performance. Employers and accreditors continue to emphasize analytical thinking, technological fluency, ethical reasoning, and accountable decision-making. Whether specific entry-level roles are shrinking, whether AI failure modes are sufficiently stable for durable curriculum, and which institutional buyers will fund this response remain market and research hypotheses to validate.

## 3. Target Users

**Students in professional degree programs.** The initial segment is third- and fourth-year undergraduates and MBA students in marketing and strategy who already hold the disciplinary foundations needed to evaluate a claim. This includes traditional students, students with internships, and part-time students with substantial work experience. Professional experience is a moderator, not an exemption: it may strengthen domain schemas and consequence awareness while also introducing overconfidence, narrow transfer, or automation bias under pressure. Students use AI frequently and may be better at eliciting a polished answer than judging whether it deserves reliance. Their immediate need is to answer the follow-up question without losing ownership of the work; the pilot must test whether repeated runs, including a first run that counts toward the course grade (7.19), are experienced as useful practice rather than punitive exposure.

**Course instructors, the buyer and enabler.** Initial users teach multiple sections, have limited assessment time, and need stronger evidence of student reasoning without creating another unmanageable workflow. Added preparation time, unclear scoring, inaccessible assessment, or a system that requires instructors to accept opaque machine judgments will prevent adoption. Today many instructors combine disclosure policies, cold-calling, secure assessments, and informal discounting of submitted work. They should switch only if Tassl produces interpretable evidence, supports rather than replaces disciplinary judgment, and reduces total assessment burden across the course. Those conditions are pilot acceptance criteria, not assumptions.

**Program and school leadership.** Associate deans, chairs, chief AI officers, assurance-of-learning leaders, and directors of teaching and learning need evidence for program outcomes, accreditation, and assessment reform. They also require privacy, accessibility, security, research, and student-appeal safeguards. Those safeguards gate the pilot. The institutional reviews behind them do not gate the build, which creates no real student data and is accepted by the walkthrough in Section 12. In the pilot, the institution is party to the written data agreement that governs Tassl's access to identified student records (Section 8); an instructor cannot grant that access alone. A pilot of one or two courses, each configured on its own free Pilot plan (Section 8), which covers one course apiece, should show feasibility, interpretable cohort patterns, faculty workload, and preliminary movement in observable behaviors; it should not be presented as proof of efficacy or a validated institutional score.

**Individual students outside an adopting course.** Students at non-adopting institutions, candidates preparing for consulting and product interviews, and graduates underwater in month two. They buy for themselves, use library scenarios, and want the record they can point to. This segment seeds institutional demand and does not drive the roadmap.

**Who Tassl is not for.** Not K-12, because the behavior presupposes disciplinary foundations. Not introductory courses, because a student new to the concepts cannot tell an unwarranted claim from an unfamiliar one, and the result is guessing that scores like judgment. Not for teaching content: Tassl requires disciplinary knowledge and reveals its absence rather than supplying it. Not for assessments with one correct answer, such as licensure prep, because the scoring model assumes more than one defensible position exists. Not for institutions wanting AI surveillance: Tassl does not detect, proctor, compare, or report. Not for massive open courses without faculty, because removing the disciplinary expert removes what makes the scores mean anything.

## 4. Value Proposition and Differentiation

Tassl is designed so that, after repeated runs, a student's reliance on AI becomes more observable and potentially better calibrated: more likely to identify consequential defects, less likely to manufacture false alarms, more likely to revise when evidence warrants and hold when it does not, and better able to explain the final decision without AI assistance. For instructors, the intended value is an evidence-linked diagnostic, read from the plotted traces of each run, that they can review efficiently. Improvement after four runs and a 12-minute review time are explicit pilot hypotheses, not established product outcomes.

Three differences are structural. **Tassl removes the artifact channel:** every alternative routes through something a capable assistant can optimize, while the Tassl decision is filed on a fixed form with hard word limits and no formatting, attachments, or images, so polish cannot earn a point because no dimension, and therefore no entry in any course's band-to-points mapping (7.19), rewards it. **Tassl controls the reliability of the AI inside the scenario:** elsewhere a student gets whatever the assistant gives them and nobody knows what the correct level of trust would have been, while inside a run the versioned claim objects (7.5) state which claims are sound and which defective, whether those objects were written by hand or generated by AI at authoring time, and in either case confirmed by a disciplinary authority (7.18), making the gap between the stance taken and the stance deserved a measured quantity. **The Decision Lock is irreversible:** the pre-change record is frozen at commitment, so Tassl distinguishes adaptive judgment from quietly rewriting the original reasoning to have predicted the outcome.

**The alternatives, including where they are stronger.** Doing nothing is free and remains the default. Secure written exams and oral examinations establish unaided knowledge and ownership better than Tassl, but are difficult to repeat and do not directly test AI-assisted judgment. Detection and proctoring answer an authorship question Tassl intentionally declines. Process-evidence tools such as Turnitin Clarity show how work was produced but do not manipulate AI reliability or consequences. AI-assessment frameworks such as the AI Assessment Scale clarify the permitted role of AI but do not provide learner-level practice. Traditional simulations such as Capsim, Markstrat, Marketplace, and Harvard Business Publishing bring mature libraries and authentic decisions; AI-native simulation platforms such as Cartedo also assess application, evidence use, decisions, and defense. Tassl must therefore differentiate on controlled calibrated-reliance instrumentation: a pre-AI baseline, known claim quality, proportional verification, confidence-versus-competence, escalation, an irreversible decision record, a Turn, an unaided defense, and a simulator-style trace readout the instructor debriefs from rather than a verdict pronounced about the student. Internships remain the strongest authentic practice environment; Tassl offers repeatable, lower-risk access when workplace opportunities are unequal or consequential practice is scarce.

## 5. Product Principles and Non-Goals

**Score the decision, never the deliverable.** Students calibrate to what is rewarded within one assignment cycle; if the best-looking output wins, AI gets pointed at appearance. Tassl caps and de-formats every submission and awards nothing for presentation. Tradeoff: no beautiful artifact to show a dean, no portfolio piece, and students who take pride in design work find the constraint frustrating for two runs.

**Preserve developmental friction; automate production friction.** Formatting, source hunting, deck assembly, and scheduling are removed. Deciding what the problem is, what to delegate, what to check under a clock, when to commit, and how to explain yourself unaided stay hard on purpose. Tradeoff: Tassl feels slower and more constrained than an open chat window, and the first run is deliberately uncomfortable.

**AI inside Tassl is useful and fallible in deliberately controlled proportions.** An assistant that is always wrong teaches suspicion; one that is always right teaches passive acceptance. The build ships paired scenario variants containing sound and plausibly flawed consequential claims: one variant with a consequential defect and one defect-free path. The pilot begins with those two variants. Proposed parameters such as 70 percent sound claims, a two-defect ceiling, and one defect-free run in six are library-level design hypotheses to calibrate with real students in the pilot, not universal properties of professional AI use. The build's two variants (Section 12) do not instantiate the one-in-six ratio; they are one defective path and one sound path, and the ratio describes the library, not the build slice. Critical claim content and verification results must be versioned and controllable so a model update cannot silently change what a run measures. This holds when the claim content is itself AI-generated: generation runs at authoring time and produces the versioned claim objects and their verification results. It never runs at a measurement moment, and regenerating a scenario creates a new version rather than altering the one in use. Tradeoff: tightly controlling claim behavior reduces open-ended realism, but measurement validity takes priority in the build and in the pilot.

**Judge against a range of defensible positions, never a single key.** Every scenario declares at least two positions a competent professional could defend and the evidence supporting each. Tradeoff: authoring takes longer even when AI drafts the scenario, because a disciplinary authority must confirm each position and each defect; scoring is harder to automate; calibration needs real cohorts; and the model is less convincing to an administrator who wants a percentage. The course's band-to-points mapping (7.19) does not supply one: it converts confirmed bands into points for that course alone, and Tassl reports no percentage, rank, or percentile of its own.

**Tassl never accuses.** No AI detection, no proctoring, no misconduct findings; inconsistencies surface to the instructor as coaching signals and stop there. Tradeoff: Tassl misses genuine dishonesty and declines an adjacent market, in exchange for being a tool students are willing to be honest inside.

**Assess like a simulator instructor, never like an examiner.** A Decision Run is recorded the way a flight simulator records a session. The instrument traces are what the student read and in what order, what they delegated, the stance on every consequential claim, each verification action and its result, confidence at the frame, at Decision Lock, and after the Turn, the clock spent by activity, the Turn response, and the defense. Tassl plots those traces as a fixed set of graphs against the authored standard, reads the seven dimension bands off the graphs, and hands graphs and bands to the instructor, who debriefs from them and confirms or changes the bands, as a flight instructor debriefs from the trace rather than from an impression of the flight. Tassl pronounces no verdict about the person; it shows what happened against what the author declared. The course's points are a mapping of the confirmed bands (7.19), not a Tassl judgment. Tradeoff: a graph readout asks more of the instructor than a number would, and a student who wants to be told whether they passed is instead shown their own trace and asked to read it.

**Faculty are the disciplinary authority.** Tassl drafts bands with the run's graphs and evidence attached; a domain expert reads the graphs, confirms, adjusts, or overrules, and their judgment is final. The bands that enter a course grade are the ones the instructor confirmed or let stand under the 14-day auto-confirmation (Section 8 lifecycle), never Tassl's unconfirmed draft. The same rule governs scenario content: when AI generates a scenario, its Readiness Check items, and its path from a published case (7.18), a disciplinary authority confirms or edits every element before any student sees it. In the build that authority is the builder; in the pilot it is the assigning faculty member. Generated content that has not been confirmed does not exist as far as a run is concerned. Tradeoff: quality is gated on the scarcest resource in the system, which caps how fast the library grows.

**Non-goals.** Tassl does not tutor, because a system that helps the student reach the answer removes the experience being developed. It does not grade essays, decks, or memos, because that reintroduces the artifact channel the first principle exists to close. It does not detect AI authorship or run similarity checks, because authorship is the wrong question and the defense answers the right one. It does not teach prompt engineering, because technique is transient while knowing what to delegate is durable. It does not simulate a market, because that is a different unit of assessment and would force the semester-length format that makes repetition impossible. It does not issue credentials; the Judgment Record is student-controlled evidence with provenance and carries no accreditation. It does not manage courses, host content, or hold the gradebook of record, because institutions already run those; it exports confirmed bands and the points the instructor's mapping produces from them (7.19) and holds no grade itself. It does not sell or broker student data; program insight is aggregate only, and nothing about an individual leaves the institution except by that individual's export. Tassl's own reading of identified pilot records for scoring audit, scenario calibration, and drift review happens under the institution's written data agreement (Section 8), within its purpose limits and role-based access, and is not a disclosure outside the institution.

## 6. End-to-End User Journey

This journey is the future state of the product across a full course. It includes Guided, Standard, and Open Modes, live stakeholder interviews, a Sycophancy Probe, a spoken defense, Critique Runs, an appeal decided by the instructor, four runs with a trajectory, and a 60-student cohort; all of these are future-state for the build. The build covers one Decision Run of this arc, and the walkthrough (Section 12, Definition of done) exercises: the Readiness Check with every result opening Standard Mode, the Scenario Brief and the Evidence Room, the frame, the constrained assistant surfacing a planted claim as an authored claim object (a stale-evidence claim where the seed case supports it), claim-level stances with confidence captured at three points, at least one verification action (a Source Trace), the Decision Lock, one Turn, a typed defense taken within the walkthrough session, seven draft bands with their evidence, the graph readout (7.13), the Run Debrief, and the faculty replay with one confirmation and one override. Guided and Open Modes, stakeholder interviews, the spoken defense, Critique Runs, the appeal, runs two through four, and the cohort are not in the walkthrough; where the walkthrough shows any of them, it shows labeled illustrative sample data, and the Sycophancy Probe appears only if the confirmed scenario contains one (7.5). The walkthrough scenario is the one generated from the seed case the builder supplies (7.18), not Meridian Roast, and the builder and the editor play the student and the faculty reviewer between them. Marco's run, the appeal, and the four-run progression below are illustrative.

Nadia Osei meets Tassl on a syllabus: four Decision Runs and four Critique Runs, 15 percent of the grade in Professor Dana Whitfield's 60-student marketing analytics course. Every run counts from the first: Whitfield's 15 percent, split at the default relative weights in 7.19 (a Critique Run at half a Decision Run), comes to 2.5 percent per Decision Run and 1.25 percent per Critique Run, this course's choice rather than a Tassl default, each computed from the confirmed dimension bands under the default band-to-points mapping. A syllabus line says the write-up earns nothing for polish; the decision, its grounds, and the follow-up questions are graded from run one. Her 8-minute, 16-item Readiness Check returns eleven correct and no score: retention and payback solid, survey error thin, no feel yet for how an assistant behaves under pushback. Her run opens in Standard Mode; a classmate who scored six opens the same scenario in Guided Mode with a primer, mandatory stance justifications, and a mandatory one-line reason on every delegation (7.6). Both runs carry the same 2.5 percent; mode changes the scaffolding around the scored core, not the core or its weight (7.1, 7.19).

Meridian Roast is an illustrative scenario. The build's scenario is adapted from a published case the builder supplies, generated by AI, and confirmed by the builder as disciplinary authority under 7.18; its company, figures, stakeholders, and defects differ from those below, and its mechanics are the same. The scenario is Meridian Roast: an $18 million subscription coffee company with a $500,000 quarterly acquisition budget, flat growth, and a founder who wants to move upmarket. Nine documents sit in the Evidence Room; two of three stakeholders contradict each other. Nadia files a Frame before the assistant unlocks: whether premium economics justify moving spend from a value tier that currently pays back, with retention, price sensitivity, and supplier cost as load-bearing assumptions, leaning toward holding at confidence 40. She locks it permanently. Over 25 minutes the assistant reads the cohort curves cleanly but pulls premium churn from an 18-month-old positioning deck rather than the retention memo that revised it, making premium payback look like 11 months rather than 16. Asked whether the tier difference is meaningful, it describes a cohort comparison competently, gives a number, and has run nothing. She accepts the payback figures untraced, then challenges the claim that the value tier is saturated; the assistant agrees without new evidence and rewrites in her favor. That interaction is an authored Sycophancy Probe, and the assistant's original position was defensible.

She moves 60 percent of budget to premium on the 11-month payback, stances it Accept because Tassl will not lock without one, and locks irreversibly. Ninety seconds later the Turn arrives: premium pilot month-three retention is 61 percent, not 78. She reverses completely. The 8-minute defense that evening asks where the payback came from, which cohort, from when, whether she checked the date, what payback becomes if churn is 20 percent worse, and why she reversed on one retention figure when her own frozen frame named supplier cost instead.

The Run Debrief is intended to be the converting experience: on the stance matrix, claim by claim, Nadia sees what she accepted, what each claim warranted under the authored conditions, the superseding memo available all along, the Source Trace that could have surfaced it, the Replication Check that could have exposed the unsupported computation, and the Probe transcript. Her confidence line, plotted at the frame, at lock, and after the Turn against the accuracy of the claims she relied on at each point, rises from 40 before AI to 85 at lock, largely on unchecked claims; the third point, after her reversal, sits beside them. Her clock timeline shows the working period with no Source Trace on the payback figure. The system reads the seven bands off those graphs and drafts them, evidence-linked, for faculty confirmation; in the build and the pilot those bands are provisional, and the pilot must test them for inter-rater consistency, fairness, and usefulness. Once Whitfield confirms them, the seven bands become the run's points under the course mapping, the mean over the assessed dimensions under the default mapping (7.19), worth 2.5 percent of Nadia's grade; run one counts exactly like run four. In run two she over-corrects, spending verification effort on claims that were already well supported. By run four she reads the Evidence Room first, escalates a consent claim she cannot evaluate, and holds through the Turn with a reason. The pilot will test whether this apparent progression reflects learning rather than scenario familiarity or knowledge of Tassl's mechanics.

Marco Bellini draws the opposite lesson: in run two he challenges 9 of 11 claims, rejects two, burns his clock, and files his pre-AI frame with hedging, catching the one consequential defect plus eight false alarms. Verification scores Professional and Calibration Novice, with a 73 percent False Challenge Rate against a professional band under 15 percent. He appeals inside the 7-day window; Whitfield reads his stance matrix and clock timeline, raises Calibration one band, noting that a wider net is defensible when information is thin, and lets stand that seven of his challenges were on claims the matrix shows he had already traced clean. ## 7. Functional Breakdown

Everything is organized around the run: one student's single pass through one scenario. The future-state Decision Run covers the full arc in approximately 60 to 90 minutes; a Critique Run is shorter and asks the student to evaluate an authored AI-assisted decision. The stable nine-stage architecture is: Know enough to begin, Frame before AI enters, Delegate deliberately, Interrogate what comes back, Calibrate reliance claim by claim, Decide under a clock, Adapt when conditions change, Defend without AI assistance, Own the outcome. The build implements the smallest complete vertical slice of this arc, defined in Section 12 under Definition of done, before any future-state capability is added. Each 7.x section below specifies the full future-state capability; where the build differs from it, an "In the build" sentence inside the section states what the build includes and what remains future-state. The pilot is the first stage with students. Scenario content changes; anchor behaviors remain stable so improvement can eventually be compared, subject to evidence that variants and modes are sufficiently comparable. Every stage writes timestamped events to one run trace, the exportable event trace of Section 12; the graphs in 7.13 are plotted from that trace and nothing else, so the Run Debrief, the Judgment Record, and the Faculty Console show the same evidence.

Six standing rules govern every capability below and are not repeated in each. When anything Tassl provides is unavailable or unusable, the clock stops and any cost spent on it is credited back. A run that cannot be scored is voided and re-offered on a fresh variant at no cost, with no partial score recorded. An unintended defect, a verification action returning a wrong result, or authored material that misbehaves neutralizes the affected claim in both directions and routes the scenario for review, with the one exception 7.2 and 7.15 state: a student who identifies a genuine unintended inconsistency is credited with a correct challenge. Generated material that a disciplinary authority has confirmed is authored material for the purpose of this rule. A dimension that cannot be assessed is reported as unassessed, never estimated, and a graph whose trace events are missing is shown as unavailable with the gap named, never reconstructed. A correction for Tassl's own error can raise a band or neutralize a dimension, never lower a band or any points computed from it. Nothing Tassl observes about a student is treated as misconduct or reported as such.

Because every run enters the course grade from run one (7.19), these rules also bound what reaches the gradebook: a voided run records no points and the re-offered run counts in its place, an unassessed or neutralized dimension is excluded from a run's points rather than counted as zero, and a correction never lowers a band or the points computed from it. In the build the rules are implemented as follows. The Paused state (Section 8, lifecycle of a run) stops the clock and credits the cost when the assistant, a document, or an interrogation action fails to return. Voiding a run, re-offering it on a fresh variant, and neutralizing a claim are actions the faculty reviewer performs from the faculty replay (7.17); each writes an event to the run trace. Unassessed dimensions and never-lower corrections are enforced in the band and points computation (7.13, 7.19). No rule is applied by editing stored data outside the interface.

### 7.1 Readiness Check and Run Modes

**How it works.** A check opens on first entry when none exists for that concept set within 21 days: 16 items in 8 minutes, six on disciplinary foundations, four on concepts the scenario's defects turn on, and six on AI behavior. Under the AI-assisted authoring path (7.18) the items are generated from the concept set the scenario declares and from the failure families its defects use, each carrying its key and the concept it maps to, and the disciplinary authority confirms or edits every item before any run uses it. The result maps named concepts rather than only a total. Guided, Standard, and Open experiences may vary scaffolding, but the scored core must retain common anchor claims, common decision conditions, and documented difficulty so mode changes are not mistaken for learning. Initial thresholds below 50, 50 to 80, and above 80 are pilot parameters requiring item analysis; students see strengths and gaps in plain language, never a rank. In the build, the Readiness Check is part of the slice: a check opens at the start of every run, including the second scored session and the short third run of the walkthrough, drawn from the same generated and confirmed 16-item set for the scenario's concept set under the reuse rule below, and the result is shown as named concepts. The 21-day suppression window and item-level retakes are future-state, so in the build no check is suppressed because an earlier one exists. The generated items have had no item analysis, so in the walkthrough both the items and the thresholds are uncalibrated and the concept map is the only result relied on; item analysis is a pilot activity. Every result opens Standard Mode in the build; routing to Guided or Open Mode, the 21-day refresh, retakes, the harder-variant offer at ceiling, and the course-wide mode floor are future-state, and the thresholds above are first tested in the pilot. The concept map is written to the run trace, shown to the student in plain language, and shown to the faculty reviewer in the replay.

**Rules.** The check's result never blocks entry; the check itself is submitted or, after a failure to complete it, skipped (Section 8, lifecycle of a run). It is used to establish whether the learner possesses the knowledge required to evaluate the scenario. Generated items enter the pool only after the disciplinary authority confirms the stem, the key, and the concept mapping; a rejected item is regenerated or written by hand, and the six, four, six split is preserved. No item names a planted defect or its location. Item analysis is a pilot activity. Scaffolding should occur before or around the common scored core rather than changing the construct being measured. Results provide context for interpreting escalation and verification, and the concept-level result on the concept a claim turns on is carried beside that claim's row in the stance matrix as context for reading the stance the student took, never as an input to what the claim warrants, which is fixed on the claim object (7.8, 7.13, 7.18); they do not justify penalizing a student more harshly for a knowledge gap, and a knowledge gap can excuse a mismatch or raise a band and can never make one count more heavily. The check is never graded and its result never enters the band-to-points mapping (7.19). Because the first Decision Run counts toward the grade, the check and Guided Mode are the only ungraded scaffolding a student receives before a counted run, which is one reason the result never blocks entry and Guided Mode is available from run one. Longitudinal comparisons must disclose mode, scenario variant, readiness, and difficulty and should not be called growth until comparability is demonstrated.

#### Edge cases and failures.

Mode-dependent cases below are future-state; in the build every check opens Standard Mode.

- Random answering to reach Open Mode: subtler defect, shorter clock; the incentive self-corrects. Open Mode's difficulty is documented with the variant (7.1 Rules) so the mode change is not mistaken for learning.
- Retake: new items from the same concept map; the latest result governs.
- Retake with fewer confirmed items than a fresh set needs: confirmed items are reused; an unconfirmed item is never drawn to fill a set.
- Generated item found defective after use (wrong key, ambiguous stem, concept mismatch): excluded from every result it touched, and no result is lowered.
- Check older than 21 days: retaken.
- Check and behavior diverge twice: instructor coaching flag.
- Two results at ceiling: the harder variant (7.14) offered.
- Accommodation: extra time, thresholds unchanged.
- Abandoned: resumes at the same item; discarded after 48 hours.
- Cannot complete: Standard Mode, competence marked unknown, escalation band widened.
- Instructor sets a course-wide mode floor: recorded as instructor-set so cross-course comparison stays honest.

### 7.2 The Scenario Brief and the Evidence Room

**How it works.** Both open when the Readiness Check closes. A brief of at most 200 words states the situation, the decision the student owns, the resources, and the deadline. The Evidence Room holds 6 to 12 documents, each dated and attributed. At least one is superseded by a later document in the same room, one presents a stakeholder's interpretation as fact, and one is accurate and irrelevant. Tassl writes to the run trace which documents were opened, in what order, for how long, and whether any preceded first use of the assistant; each open and close is an event in the exportable trace (Section 12), carrying the document id, the duration, and whether it preceded the first delegation, and together these events form the reading segment of the clock timeline (7.13). No hints, highlighting, recommended order, or summary.

**Rules.** Every claim a defensible decision rests on is traceable inside the room. Nothing is hidden or locked, documents run at most 4 pages, and the company, market, and people are fictional but plausible, blocking any lookup of a real answer. When a scenario is adapted from a published case (7.18), the adaptation re-skins the case so its teaching note cannot resolve the scenario: fictional company, market, and people names; altered figures, so that no number in the published case or its teaching note resolves a consequential claim; and restructured evidence, so that the documents do not mirror the case's exhibits in content or order. Before confirmation the disciplinary authority checks the teaching note's recommended answer and figures against the confirmed answer space and claim objects. The published case may be licensed; adapting it is subject to the publisher's terms, and any attribution the license requires is recorded in the authoring record and instructor-facing material, never in student-facing material, because naming the seed case would enable the lookup this rule forbids. This is a flag for the author to resolve, not a legal opinion.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Never opening the room: permitted; shows in Delegation and Verification, and hardest in the defense, where every provenance question gets the same answer.
- Four-second opens: read against document length and reported as skimming, with no inference about intent.
- Asking the assistant to summarize the room: legitimate, logged as a delegation; the summary carries stances and flattens the contradictions that matter.
- Imported outside facts about a resembling real company: not penalized, and become assumptions to defend.
- Student recognizes the published case the scenario was adapted from: not penalized; the re-skinned figures and evidence do not match the teaching note, and any figure carried over from the case is an assumption to defend, with the defense asking where it came from. If a carried-over figure or the teaching note's conclusion does resolve a consequential claim, the adaptation failed: the claim is neutralized under the standing rules and the scenario is returned for a deeper re-skin.
- Genuine unintended inconsistency found: credited as a correct challenge.
- Unintended contradiction that changed decisions: every affected run re-scored under the correction rule, which can raise a band or neutralize a dimension and never lowers a band or the points computed from it.

### 7.3 Stakeholder Interviews

**How it works.** In the build, live stakeholder interviews are future-state (Section 12). Each stakeholder's position, incentives, and blind spots are defined in the confirmed scenario (7.18) and reach the student as at least one dated, attributed document in the Evidence Room, including the document that presents a stakeholder's interpretation as fact (7.2), and through the Turn where the scenario's Turn is a stakeholder message (7.11); the two stakeholders who contradict each other do so across their documents. The Stakeholder Interview action is not offered, no interview clock cost is charged, and the cap of three exchanges per stakeholder is inactive. A stakeholder claim read from a document requires a stance like any other. The specification that follows stands for the pilot and later. Available from scenario open until Decision Lock and again during the Turn window. The student asks a named stakeholder a question in their own words and gets an answer in voice, consistent with the position, incentives, and blind spots defined in the confirmed scenario, whether written by hand or generated and confirmed under 7.18. Stakeholders do not lie outright; they emphasize selectively, are confident about what they have not checked, and at least two contradict each other on a point that matters. Each interview costs 4 minutes of clock, capped at three exchanges per stakeholder.

**Rules.** Stakeholders never reveal the answer, never confirm or deny whether an AI claim is defective, and never comment on performance. A stakeholder claim requires a stance like any other.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Asked to decide: in-role deflection and a statement that the call is the student's.
- Same question to every stakeholder: a genuinely different answer from each.
- Attempts to break character: in-character responses, no acknowledgment.
- 12 minutes of interviews: half the clock gone; the debrief says whether that paid.
- No interviews where the decisive information lives only in a stakeholder's head: an error the debrief names.
- Garbled or out-of-character response: discarded, excluded from scoring.

### 7.4 Framing and the Independent First Position

**How it works.** Framing is the most important control in the product: without a position captured before AI enters, nothing distinguishes the student's reasoning from the assistant's. It is mandatory once the Evidence Room opens and before the assistant unlocks. The student states the real decision in at most 50 words, three load-bearing assumptions at 25 words each, an initial position of 100 words that may be a lean, and a confidence number from 0 to 100. Tassl locks it permanently and unlocks the assistant without evaluating or commenting. The frame stays visible for the rest of the run.

**Rules.** The frame is immutable, every field must be filled before the assistant unlocks, and it is the baseline for Adaptation scoring and the left panel of the frame-beside-decision graph (7.13).

#### Edge cases and failures.

- One word per field: passes the gate; costs the student twice, in Framing and in the defense.
- Honest "I do not know" with a reason and confidence under 30: credited above a confident guess.
- A different framing from the author's: assessed against an answer space holding more than one; framings outside it are named with the evidence they missed.
- Frame written with an outside assistant: follow-ups three levels deep in the defense, where the cost lands; Tassl neither detects nor accuses.
- Assumption named in advance that the Turn later disrupts: credited in Adaptation.
- Frame lost before locking: re-entered. Locked frame: never restored, edited, or replaced, including by an instructor.

### 7.5 The AI Assistant

**How it works.** Available from frame lock to Decision Lock and again during the Turn window, but never during the defense, the assistant works inside the scenario's world and can read, summarize, compute, compare, draft, and argue. Consequential claims must be represented as versioned claim objects with authored evidence status and acceptable verification paths. Under the AI-assisted authoring path (7.18) generation produces these objects at authoring time, each carrying its text, its evidence status (sound or defective), its failure family where defective, its source document and passage, its warranted stance, the verification actions that can expose it and the result each action returns, and the delegation triggers that surface it. The disciplinary authority confirms the objects and they are frozen with a version before the walkthrough; no claim content is generated at a measurement moment, and a change to a claim object creates a new scenario version. The build uses frozen or constrained responses at measurement moments, with generative language around the controlled claim layer; the pilot may keep the same structure. When a delegation matches a trigger, the assistant's response carries the claim text verbatim and the interface presents that claim as a discrete item with its stance control, its Source Trace, and its other available actions; the surrounding generative text carries no stance and is never scored as consequential. The assistant cannot introduce a consequential claim that is not in the confirmed scenario; if the faculty reviewer finds one in the replay, that claim is neutralized under the standing rules and the scenario is routed for review. A Sycophancy Probe is an authored claim object with a scripted reversal and is included only if the confirmed scenario contains one; the walkthrough does not require it. Proposed reliability parameters and Sycophancy Probe frequency are calibrated per scenario rather than assumed globally; in the build they are set by the confirming authority and are uncalibrated. Unintended model claims are excluded or neutralized under the standing rules.

**Rules.** Ten durable failure families guide authored defects: answering a near-neighbor question; relying on an unstated assumption; using stale evidence; presenting a number as computed when nothing was; extrapolating beyond evidence; reversing to agree without evidence; omitting a materially better alternative; misapplying a legitimate method; attributing a claim to a source that does not support it; and reaching a plausible result through an ethically or organizationally unacceptable route. These are a curriculum taxonomy to validate and refresh, not a claim that model behavior is stable. The assistant is labeled AI assistant, does not reveal defect locations or scores, and does not produce the Decision Brief. Every planted defect must be consequential, traceable, and confirmed by a disciplinary expert; this confirmation is content review, not an institutional approval, and in the build the expert is the builder acting as disciplinary authority. A generated defect is no exception: it enters a run only after the disciplinary authority has confirmed its failure family, the evidence that exposes it, and the decision it changes.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Never used: Delegation scored on whether that choice was reasoned and stated, usually poor in a computation-driven scenario, sometimes excellent in a short judgment call.
- Asked for the whole answer at once: returns a complete, stance-requiring answer, which is the correct test.
- Attempts to extract defect locations by question, role-play, or self-audit: ordinary in-scenario responses, recorded neutrally; repeated attempts surface to the instructor.
- Asked to check its own earlier answer: may or may not catch the problem, so Replication Check exists separately.
- Another language: fluent assistant, identical scoring.
- Invented information supplied by the student: answered on its own terms; the premise becomes theirs to defend.
- Offensive or out-of-scenario content: flagged in one action, excluded from scoring.
- Generated claim object whose source passage does not support its assigned evidence status: removed or corrected before confirmation; found after a run, neutralized under the standing rules and the scenario routed for review.

### 7.6 The Delegation Log and Outside-Tool Declaration

**How it works.** The log populates from the moment the assistant unlocks, recording in the run trace each delegation as a unit of work rather than a chat transcript: what was asked, what came back, which claims resulted, which the student used, and what stance each carried. One optional line states why, required in Guided Mode; the Guided Mode requirement is future-state with the modes, so in the build the line is always optional. A standing control lets the student declare outside-tool use and its purpose. The log and the declaration control are in the build; in the build the control is a text field that writes one event to the run trace and has no scoring effect. Each delegation appears as a segment on the clock timeline, and the claims it produced as rows in the stance matrix (7.13).

**Rules.** Declaring outside-tool use never lowers a band or a point in the course mapping (7.19), in any mode or course, and this is stated in the interface, since a declaration carrying a penalty produces silence. Tassl does not detect, infer, or estimate undeclared use. Course policy governs whether outside use is permitted.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Undeclared outside use: questioned on it from their own record; the cost appears in the defense or not at all.
- Everything declared as outside: taken at face value; the thin in-environment log is reported as fact, not judgment.
- Declaration inside an In-Environment Only course: surfaced to the instructor with no scoring effect; what follows is their call.
- Outside text pasted into the brief: not blocked, and every claim in it must be stanced before lock.
- Incomplete log: Delegation scored from the defense alone.

### 7.7 Interrogation Actions

**How it works.** Four actions are available on any claim from its creation until Decision Lock and again during the Turn window. A Source Trace, 1 minute, shows the document a claim came from, the passage it rests on, and that document's date and author. A Replication Check, 3 minutes, independently re-derives an analytical claim and shows both results. A Decomposition Check, 4 minutes, splits a multi-step conclusion into steps and shows each result. A Stakeholder Interview costs 4 minutes. Tassl never says a claim is wrong; it shows what the action produced, and records the action, its cost, and its result on the clock timeline and in the stance matrix (7.13). In the build, Source Trace is the required verification action and works on every sourced claim, because the defective variant's planted defect is stale evidence where the seed case supports it (Section 12) and Source Trace is the action that surfaces it. Replication Check and Decomposition Check are built only where the confirmed path (7.18) names one of them as the acceptable verification path for a planted claim; otherwise they are future-state, are not offered in the run, and the debrief names only the actions that were available. The Stakeholder Interview action is future-state with 7.3. Every action's result is shown as produced, with its clock cost charged at the moment the action starts.

**Rules.** The working clock is the only constraint on Source Trace, Replication Check, and Decomposition Check, since a quota would tell students how much checking is correct; stakeholder exchanges (7.3) and escalations (7.9) carry their own caps. Actions cannot be undone, and a Replication Check runs independently rather than asking the assistant to re-examine itself. For a consequential claim, the result each action returns is part of the versioned claim object (7.5), authored or generated and then confirmed; it is not produced by the assistant at run time.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Replication Check that agrees: reasonable on a load-bearing claim, 3 wasted minutes otherwise.
- 11 actions and a buzzer-beating file: the over-verification failure, which the debrief quantifies against the two claims that mattered.
- Zero actions, everything accepted: scores well in a defect-free run.
- Traced the claim, misread the date: right action, wrong conclusion; the debrief separates them, since they need different coaching.
- Decomposition Check on a single-step claim: 4 minutes spent on nothing.
- Clock expires mid-action: the result still returns; an action once started completes.

### 7.8 Reliance Stances and Confidence

**How it works.** A stance states, before the outcome is known, how the student will use a claim. A claim may come from the assistant, a stakeholder (7.3), an Evidence Room document, or material the student brought in (7.6); 7.13 calls the document case an Evidence Room stance. Accept relies on it as it stands. Verify relies after a meaningful check. Challenge identifies a potentially material problem or omission. Reject declines to use it. Escalate identifies a limit in the student's competence or authority. Tassl records stance, timing, evidence available, verification cost, claim importance, learner readiness, and consequence level. The comparison is not simply whether a stance matched a correct/incorrect label; it asks whether the action was proportionate to reliability, decision importance, consequences of error, cost of checking, and the learner's evaluative competence. Tassl plots this comparison as the stance matrix (7.13): one row per consequential claim, the stance taken against the stance warranted. The warranted stance is a fixed attribute of each claim object (7.18): authored or generated from that claim's evidence status, importance, consequence level, and verification cost, then confirmed by the disciplinary authority and versioned with the claim. It is the same for every student who meets that claim, which is what lets the stance matrix compare one run with another. The student's Readiness Check result on the concept the claim turns on is carried beside the row as context for reading the stance, not as an input that changes what the claim warrants, so a knowledge gap can excuse a mismatch or raise a band and can never make a mismatch count more heavily against the student (7.1). Confidence is captured in the frame, at lock, and after the Turn; those three points are the confidence line (7.13), plotted against the authored accuracy of the claims relied on at each point.

**Rules.** Language or a number tracing to an unstanced consequential claim prevents lock until a stance is set. Operationally, a consequential claim is relied on when any of the following holds: the student marked it as used in the Delegation Log (7.6); a number it carries is entered in a named numeric field of the Decision Brief (7.10); or it surfaced in the Turn window (7.11). A relied-on claim without a stance prevents lock, and the refusal names the first such claim. Claims surfaced but not relied on may remain unstanced and are reported as such in the replay. Correct escalation is valued highly, but blanket or avoidant escalation is not automatically superior to appropriate acceptance. Over-verification, under-verification, over-challenge, and passive acceptance are all potential calibration failures. A sound claim may still warrant verification when it is load-bearing, high-consequence, cheap to check, weakly sourced, volatile, or outside the learner's competence.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Accept everything: bottom of Calibration in a two-defect run, top in a defect-free run.
- Verify everything without actions: recorded as asserted verification, and the defense asks how one specific claim was verified.
- Both escalations spent on claims inside demonstrated competence: over-escalation, which scores as under-confidence; the two-escalation limit (7.9) makes escalating everything impossible.
- Stance changed after an action: exactly right; both stances are kept.
- Constant 100 percent confidence: answered by showing the student their own confidence line (7.13), three flat points against the accuracy of the claims they relied on.
- Flat 50 percent: uninformative, and scores as poorly as a badly miscalibrated line.
- More than a third of consequential claims lose their stance record: the run cannot be scored, so under the standing rules it is voided and re-offered on a fresh variant at no cost, with no points recorded for the voided run and the re-offered run counting in its place. Where a third or fewer are lost, the run stands and both dimensions read from the stance matrix, Calibration and Verification, are reported as unassessed and excluded from the run's points rather than counted as zero (7.13, 7.19).

### 7.9 Escalation

**How it works.** Available on any claim before Decision Lock and during the Turn window, the student names the claim, selects Escalate, and says in one sentence what they cannot evaluate. A senior colleague responds within the run, costs 5 minutes of clock, and gives a partial answer: enough to unblock, hedged where hedging is honest, explicitly incomplete. The colleague sometimes says the student should have worked this out, and sometimes says this was exactly the right thing to ask. In the build, Escalate is available as a stance on any claim, and the colleague's reply is an authored partial answer stored on each escalatable consequential claim object in the confirmed path (7.18), consistent with the constrained claim layer in 7.5, confirmed by the disciplinary authority, returned verbatim, charged 5 minutes, and subject to the two-per-run limit. An escalation on a claim with no authored reply returns the path's general reply, is charged 5 minutes, and does not count against the limit. A generative colleague that responds to arbitrary requests, with live escalation dialogue, is future-state.

**Rules.** The initial design permits two escalations per run, a parameter to test rather than a fixed professional norm. The colleague never decides or reveals defect status. Escalation is scored in context: what the student could know, the consequences, the expertise or authority missing, the specificity of the request, and how the response was used. Correct escalation can be professional performance; indiscriminate escalation is under-confidence or avoidance.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Escalated correctly, then accepted the hedge without noticing it: a success followed by a failure, separated in the debrief.
- Both consequential defects escalated: top of Calibration even with no personal detection, since what matters is that they never reached the decision.
- Escalated on the last minute: an answer with no time to use it, scored as timing rather than judgment.
- Escalating unread claims as hint-seeking: answers that identify nothing, 10 minutes gone.
- Guided Mode: fuller answers, since foundations need teaching rather than unblocking; the fuller answer is scaffolding recorded against the mode, and the escalation is scored on the same claim, request, and use as in every mode (7.1). Adaptive modes are future-state (Section 12).
- Response fails to arrive: does not count against the limit of two.

### 7.10 The Decision Brief and Decision Lock

**How it works.** The brief is where the student commits: deliberately small, ugly, and unformattable, openable at any time and filed before the working clock expires. It holds a recommendation of at most 120 words, a rationale of at most 250, three assumptions at 25 words each, 60 words on what would change their mind, and a confidence number, with central numbers typed into named numeric fields rather than pasted. The named fields for a scenario are declared in the confirmed path (7.18); in the Section 6 journey, for example, they would be the share of budget moved and the payback months. A value entered in a named field that matches a value carried by a claim object marks that claim as relied on (7.8). Tassl refuses the lock until every claim the brief relies on carries a stance, naming the one at fault, then freezes the brief, frame, delegation log, stance record, and interrogation history as an immutable pre-Turn record. The pre-Turn record is the left side of the frame-beside-decision graph (7.13). The working clock is a per-scenario package field (7.18) running from frame lock to Decision Lock; its initial default is 25 minutes, a pilot parameter, and in the build it is marked uncalibrated. Stakeholder interviews taken before frame lock are charged against it. The working clock and the Turn window run only while the run is open, which is why a closed run can reach Abandoned after 72 hours (Section 8).

**Rules.** No attachments, images, tables, formatting, or links, with no exceptions, and word limits are hard. The brief is never scored on writing quality, structure, or tone; it is read for what decision was made, on what grounds, against which assumptions. The lock is irreversible, since the measurement of adaptation depends on it. One lock precedes the Turn and one follows it.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Clock expires unfiled: auto-locks whatever exists, empty fields recorded empty and any consequential claim the brief relies on that carries no stance recorded as unstanced, not as Accept; the run continues to the Turn and defense, and the debrief treats the gap as time management.
- 4-minute lock: flagged as a speed outlier, a signal rather than a penalty, with an identical defense.
- Post-lock edit attempted: refused; an Addendum is offered, 50 timestamped words visible to the student and to every reviewer of the run and never part of the original decision.
- Recommendation outside the answer space: bottom of Decision Quality, with the debrief naming the evidence it ignores.
- Recommendation identical to the assistant's: not penalized, since identical positions are often correct.
- Brief declining to recommend: assessed on whether that is true, which it usually is not; the answer space states the minimum defensible commitment.
- Failed lock: student returned to the brief with content preserved.

### 7.11 The Turn and Adaptation

**How it works.** The Turn fires automatically 60 to 120 seconds after Decision Lock, once per Decision Run. One piece of new information arrives in the voice of the world: a stakeholder message, a corrected number, a supplier notice, a competitor move, a retracted source, a regulatory note. It is genuinely new, never a hint. The assistant, Evidence Room, and interrogation actions reopen for 12 minutes, in which any new claim requires a stance. The student responds hold, revise, or reverse, with at most 150 words of justification and an updated confidence number, their frozen pre-Turn record alongside in the same frame-beside-decision layout the debrief and the console later show (7.13).

**Rules.** Roughly one Turn in three does not warrant a change of decision, a library-level ratio maintained deliberately across the library once more than one scenario exists; the build's two variants each carry one Turn and do not instantiate the ratio. Holding with a stated reason scores exactly as highly as warranted revision, and over-adaptation scores as poorly as failing to adapt. Adaptation is assessed against the frozen frame, and a defensible decision the Turn later undermines remains defensible. Whether a Turn warrants hold, revise, or reverse, and what the proportionate response is (hold, marginal revision, or reversal) with the evidence for that judgment, is declared in the confirmed path (7.18) before any run; Adaptation is drafted against that declaration and the frozen frame. In the build that declaration rests on the disciplinary authority's confirmation of a generated Turn, with no calibration cohort behind it.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- No response in the window: auto-locks the original as an implicit hold with no justification. Where the Turn did not warrant a change, Adaptation is mid-band for the hold and bottom for the missing justification, with Appendix A fixing which interior band the hold reaches; where it did, the run failed to adapt and sits at the bottom of Adaptation.
- Full reversal on information warranting a marginal adjustment: over-adaptation; the debrief quantifies the mismatch.
- Revision that never mentions the Turn: scored on substance and flagged for the defense.
- Claim to have anticipated the Turn: checked live against the frozen frame, which the lock makes unfakeable.
- Offline when the Turn fires: delivered on next entry with the full 12 minutes.
- Turn contradicting something verified before lock: the intended hard case; holding and revising are both defensible.
- Turn later found miscalibrated: re-authored, affected Adaptation bands neutralized, which removes Adaptation from those runs' points rather than counting it as zero (7.19); affected runs re-export.

### 7.12 The Defense

**How it works.** The defense is the only stage after the assistant unlocks in which AI assistance is unavailable; the Readiness Check and the frame also precede it. It opens after the Turn response locks and is completed within 48 hours, a pilot parameter matching the Defense Missed transition in Section 8; in the walkthrough it is taken within the walkthrough session. Six to nine adaptive questions are drawn from the student's own run trace (7.13): provenance, verification choices, assumptions, confidence, and the relationship between the frozen frame and revision. They are drawn against a per-scenario question bank that is part of the confirmed path (7.18): under the AI-assisted authoring path it is generated with the scenario and confirmed by the disciplinary authority, and run-time selection chooses from the confirmed bank and fills in the student's own claims, figures, and stances. Adaptive means that each question is bound to a condition in the run trace and is asked only when the condition holds. In the build: a consequential claim relied on without a Source Trace draws its provenance question; a claim whose stance changed after an action draws its verification question; a frame assumption that the brief or the Turn response departs from draws its assumption question; the confidence question is asked when confidence at lock exceeds confidence at frame; the frame-versus-response question is asked after any Turn response. Selection asks at least six and at most nine; when fewer than six conditions hold, the bank's default questions fill the set. Each question carries one authored follow-up, asked when the typed answer names no source, number, or reason, and expected-answer notes the faculty seat reads against; deeper follow-up is future-state. The initial experience targets approximately eight minutes, but duration and modality are pilot variables. The defense tests ownership and transfer of reasoning, while the research protocol must examine whether memory, anxiety, language, speech, processing speed, or interface demands introduce construct-irrelevant variance.

**Rules.** The assistant and Evidence Room are unavailable; the locked brief, the frozen frame (7.4), and the Turn response remain visible. Spoken and typed pathways must be available where modality is not the learning objective; both use equivalent follow-up logic and are analyzed for comparability rather than presumed equivalent. The build implements the typed pathway only, with questions drawn from the run trace and the confirmed question bank; the spoken pathway is future-state and the comparability study is pilot work. Fluency, accent, grammar, and pace are excluded from rubrics. A Coherence Gap is a coaching hypothesis when strong recorded reasoning is not supported in defense; it is not evidence of misconduct and carries no automatic penalty.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- "I do not know": credited when accurate and paired with what they would do about it; the follow-up tests honesty.
- Brief read aloud verbatim: triggers a follow-up that cannot be answered from the brief.
- Nothing answered: low Ownership and an instructor flag; the debrief separates not knowing the content from not knowing one's own work.
- Dropped connection: resumes at the same question within 10 minutes, or reschedules with new questions.
- Second language: scored on content alone.
- Poorly scored decision defended well: Decision Quality moves up.
- Defense taken at hour 47 after reconstructing the reasoning: legitimate, recorded as timing; preparation is not cheating.
- Window missed: run marked Defense Missed, Ownership capped at the lowest band, other dimensions intact, reopenable once. Ownership counts at Novice in the run's points rather than being excluded, because a missed window is the student's outcome and not a Tassl failure; a defense completed after the one reopening re-scores the run.
- Unusable transcript: defense re-offered with a new question set.

### 7.13 Judgment Scoring

**How it works.** After the defense, Tassl plots the run trace as the graph set below and reads evidence across seven provisional dimensions in four descriptive bands: Novice, Developing, Proficient, and Professional. Framing concerns the decision and load-bearing assumptions. Delegation concerns what was handed over and retained. Verification concerns whether checks were relevant and capable of changing the decision. Calibration concerns whether reliance was proportionate to advice quality, stakes, verification cost, and evaluative competence, with confidence interpreted separately. Decision Quality concerns defensibility against evidence available at the time. Adaptation concerns proportional response to the Turn. Ownership concerns the ability to explain the final decision and AI's role without AI assistance. Each draft band carries its evidence: the graph it was read from and the trace events behind it. Tassl produces no composite judgment score, rank, percentile, or validated trait claim about the student, in the build or the pilot: its output for a run is the assessed bands with their evidence, and nothing else. A course may convert confirmed bands into gradebook points through the instructor-set band-to-points mapping in 7.19. Those points are a course grading artifact: Tassl performs the arithmetic the instructor's mapping specifies and exports the result as the course's arithmetic, but it does not present the result as a measure of judgment, store it in the Judgment Record (7.16), or compare it across students, sections, or courses. The draft descriptors for all four bands of each dimension, and the three interior boundaries between them, are in Appendix A. They are drafted only from placements this document already fixes; the builder edits the three interior boundaries of each dimension before the build, and the pilot calibrates them afterward. Until pilot calibration a boundary is a descriptive draft against the authored standard, not a validated cut score.

**Grading model.** Tassl grades a run the way a flight simulator's instructor grades a session, and the pipeline has five steps in a fixed order: trace, graphs, bands, mapping, points. The run trace is the timestamped record of what the student did: documents opened and in what order, each delegation, the stance on every consequential claim and when it was set, each interrogation action with its cost and result, each escalation, confidence at the frame, at lock, and after the Turn, the locked brief, the Turn response, and the defense transcript. Tassl plots the trace as the graph set below against the authored standard: the answer space, the authored evidence status, importance, consequence level, and verification cost of each claim object, and whether the Turn warranted a change. The seven dimension bands are read off the graphs, and each draft band cites the graph and the trace events it rests on. The instructor reads the graphs the way a flight instructor reads a simulator trace, debriefs from them, and confirms or changes the bands; their decision is final. The course's band-to-points mapping (7.19) converts confirmed bands into gradebook points. Tassl stops at the bands: it pronounces no verdict about the person and produces no composite judgment score, rank, or percentile. The points are a course grading artifact computed from the bands by the instructor's mapping, not a Tassl claim about the student.

**The graph set.** Tassl presents a run as plotted evidence and shows what happened against the authored standard. Four graphs are plotted per run, from the run trace and nothing else, and every graph is drawn from the exportable event trace (Section 12). The Run Debrief (7.14), the Judgment Record (7.16), and the faculty replay in the Faculty Console (7.17) show these same four from the same trace, so student, record, and instructor look at identical evidence. Every graph carries its underlying data table and a text description (7.20). No graph carries a composite, rank, or percentile.

- Confidence line: confidence at the frame, at lock, and after the Turn, plotted against the authored accuracy of the claims the student relied on at each point, that is, the share of relied-on consequential claims at each point that were sound or verified under the authored conditions. The confidence reading of Calibration comes from it, interpreted separately from the stance record.
- Clock timeline: the working clock and the Turn window as a timeline segmented by activity: Evidence Room reading by document, stakeholder interviews, delegations, each interrogation action, each escalation, time in the brief, unattributed time, and the Turn response, with each claim-touching event and each clock-stop credit marked. Delegation and Verification read from it.
- Stance matrix: one row per consequential claim, showing the stance taken (Accept, Verify, Challenge, Reject, or Escalate) against the stance warranted under the authored conditions (7.8), when the stance was set, the verification action that preceded it if any, and the claim's authored evidence status and importance, with load-bearing claims marked. Beneath the rows, a five-by-five summary counts stances taken against stances warranted, and the False Challenge Rate is shown, computed as the number of consequential claims whose authored evidence status is sound and whose warranted stance is Accept or Verify that the student stanced Challenge or Reject, divided by the count of consequential claims in the run, which reproduces the Section 6 arithmetic of eight false alarms over eleven claims as 73 percent. Proportionality is already carried by the warranted stance, so no separate judgment of disproportion enters the computation. Verification and Calibration read from it, and False Challenge Rate and Calibration Gain (Section 10) are computed from it.
- Frame beside decision: the frozen frame (decision, three assumptions, position, confidence) beside the locked brief (recommendation, three assumptions, what would change their mind, confidence) and beside the Turn response (hold, revise, or reverse, justification, confidence), with the assumptions the Turn disrupted marked. Decision Quality and Adaptation read from it, and Framing reads from it together with the reading segment of the clock timeline, since a frame is banded partly on the evidence the student read before the assistant unlocked (Appendix A.1). A dimension that reads from more than one graph is reported unassessed when any graph it reads from is unavailable, and its draft band cites every graph it was read from.

Ownership is read from the defense transcript against the four graphs, not from a graph of its own. A fifth graph, the cross-run trajectory (the seven bands per run, the calibration curve, the False Challenge Rate trend, and confidence against accuracy across runs), needs more than one run; in the build it shows the one live run's bands, and a four-run trajectory appears only as illustrative sample data labeled as such (Section 12). It becomes live in the pilot.

**Interpretation to confirm.** The Open Questions answer on how grading works, "an assessor, research on graphs, think like a pilot simulator", is read in this document as follows: Tassl is the assessor; it records the run as instrument traces and presents them as plotted graphs rather than as a verdict; the instructor debriefs from the graphs as a flight instructor debriefs from a simulator trace; the bands are read off the graphs against the authored standard; and the course's points are a mapping of the bands. If "research on graphs" meant something else, for example an assessor that searches the student's path through the scenario's decision graph, a checkride in which named critical errors fail the run outright, a single assessor readout that replaces the seven dimensions, or the instructor as sole assessor with no draft bands at all, correct this paragraph and the graph set before the build begins. Which four graphs the set contains is the editor's construction rather than something the answer fixed. Delete this paragraph once confirmed.

**Rules.** Tassl plots the graphs, drafts an evidence summary, and proposes a provisional band per dimension; a human with disciplinary expertise reads the graphs and confirms or changes consequential judgments. In the walkthrough that human is the faculty reviewer role, which confirms or changes the draft bands to exercise the path; the bands are read against a scenario that has not been field calibrated and are demo-grade. In the pilot, faculty with disciplinary expertise confirm or change consequential judgments. The band descriptors are the drafts in Appendix A, pending the builder's edit of the three interior boundaries and then pilot calibration. In the build the evidence attached to a band is the graph it was read from and the list of trace events it rests on. Every band is drafted against the Appendix A descriptors from two kinds of input. The categorical part is computed from recorded events and authored claim attributes: stances against warranted stances, actions and their results, reading order and clock spend, the hold, revise, or reverse category against the Turn's authored warrant, and confidence at the three points. The rest is free text that a descriptor turns on, and wherever a descriptor turns on free text the model reads it, quotes what it read as evidence, and the band is shown as provisional. The free-text reads are: the locked frame's decision, assumptions, and position, read against the answer space and the Evidence Room, for Framing; the optional why line and any stated reason for not delegating, for Delegation; the recommendation and rationale, read against the answer space, for Decision Quality; the Turn justification, read against the frozen frame, for Adaptation; and the typed defense answers, read against the question bank's expected-answer notes, for Ownership. Verification and Calibration are computed throughout. A band whose free-text read the run does not support, such as Delegation on an incomplete log, is drafted from the defense answers instead (7.6) and marked provisional on that basis. A dimension for which the run holds no evidence is shown as unassessed. The pilot must measure inter-rater agreement, override patterns, review time, subgroup differences, and whether faculty can explain decisions to students. Double-rating and override review in the pilot are done on identified run records under the written data agreement (Section 8). Decision Quality is assessed without hindsight, and communication quality is separated from domain reasoning and reliance behavior. Only confirmed bands enter the point mapping, whether confirmed by the instructor or by the 14-day auto-confirmation in the run lifecycle (Section 8); no points are computed from a draft band, and no draft band reaches the gradebook of record. Draft bands do appear elsewhere, always labeled draft: beside the provisional points in the debrief (7.14), as draft_band events in the exportable event trace (Section 12), and in a Practice Pass record and its export, which carry draft bands and say so (7.16, Section 8). Because every run enters the course grade from run one, the standing rules bind the grade directly: a voided run records no points and the re-offered run counts in its place; a dimension that cannot be assessed is excluded from the run's points, never estimated and never counted as zero; a correction for Tassl's own error can raise a band or neutralize a dimension and never lowers a band or the points computed from it (7.19). Risk-based review (7.17) requires individual human attention for flags, band boundaries, appeals, and a random audit sample, and lets the instructor confirm the remainder in a batch; the queue and batch confirmation are pilot features and are not in the build. Because every run carries grade weight from run one and batch-confirmed bands become points, a batch-confirmed run is labeled batch-confirmed in the debrief, the export, and the Judgment Record; human validation means the graphs were read, and the product must not imply that an unread batch confirmation constitutes individual human validation.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- No consequential AI claims: Delegation scored on the justification for not delegating, Calibration on Evidence Room stances.
- Nothing caught because there was nothing to catch: high Calibration, with the stance matrix showing Accept as the warranted stance on every row and the debrief explaining why.
- Novice across every dimension: instructor flag and a recommendation to revisit foundations, since repetition without knowledge produces nothing.
- Override rate outside 10 to 25 percent: surfaced to the instructor as information rather than criticism.
- Draft scoring cannot complete but the run record is intact: run held pending, instructor notified with the raw trace and whichever graphs could be plotted, and able to band the run manually with any dimension the record cannot support marked unassessed (7.17); the student is told it is under review rather than shown a partial result; no points export until the run is Confirmed. A run whose record cannot support scoring at all is voided and re-offered under the standing rules, with no points recorded.
- Trace events missing for one graph: that graph is shown as unavailable with the missing events named, the dimensions read from it are reported unassessed, and the other graphs and bands stand.

### 7.14 The Run Debrief

**How it works.** The debrief is delivered within 10 minutes of draft scoring, with every band marked draft until the instructor confirms; confirmed bands and any instructor note replace the draft in place. Opening the debrief and answering its two questions is required before the next run unlocks and does not wait for confirmation. It embeds the graph set of 7.13 in the same form the Faculty Console shows and the Judgment Record stores, and walks the run in order: the frame-beside-decision graph, with the frame next to the final decision; the stance matrix, every consequential claim with what the student did, what it deserved, and why, including the ones they got right; the defects missed, where the disconfirming evidence sat, and which interrogation action, at what clock cost, would have surfaced each, marked on the matrix rows; the Sycophancy Probe verbatim if one fired; the confidence line, confidence at the frame, at lock, and after the Turn against the accuracy of the claims relied on; the Turn, the response, and the frozen frame side by side on the frame-beside-decision graph; the clock timeline by activity, minute by minute; and a three-sentence counterfactual of a professional stance. The counterfactual and each claim's statement of what it deserved and why are part of the confirmed scenario package (7.18): under the AI-assisted authoring path they are generated with the claim objects and confirmed, not composed at run time. Sections a single run cannot support, such as a Sycophancy Probe when none was authored or a cross-run comparison, are named as missing rather than filled. Then the bands (seven for a Decision Run, three for a Critique Run), each pointing to the graph it was read from and the evidence behind it, marked provisional until the instructor confirms them, with the course's band-to-points mapping, this run's weight, and the provisional points the draft bands would produce under that mapping shown beside them, the points labeled draft, so the student can see what the run will be worth once confirmed (7.19), and two written questions: which single stance they would change, and what they will do differently next run, both shown back at the start of that run. A dispute flagged from a draft debrief goes to the instructor's queue and is decided at confirmation; after confirmation it is an appeal under the 7-day window (Section 8). In the build, the single-run debrief with the graph readout is exercised in the walkthrough twice, once with draft bands after scoring and again after the faculty reviewer role confirms or overrides them in the faculty replay; there is no next run to lock, the two written answers are recorded in the event trace rather than shown back, and the next-run lock and the shown-back answers are exercised in the pilot.

**Rules.** The debrief never uses the word cheating and never characterizes motives; failures are attributed to specific actions and omissions. It always names at least one thing done well, since an entirely negative debrief stops being read by run three. It is permanently available to the student and to the course's reviewers (the instructor, and a teaching assistant for their section) for the life of the record under 7.16, and is never shown to other students; in the pilot the Tassl Scenario Editor may read it as part of the identified run record under the written data agreement (Section 8). Student and instructor see the same graphs plotted from the same trace; the debrief never renders a run differently from the console or the record.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Professional across all seven: shows the harder variant coming next and the one action closest to a mistake, marked on the stance matrix.
- Novice across all seven: leads with the two concepts underneath the failures rather than the failures.
- Debrief skipped: the next run stays locked until it is opened and both questions answered; an answer stating disagreement routes to the appeal path.
- Claim-level judgment disputed: flagged from inside the debrief in one action, creating an appeal without a separate process.
- Partial debrief: delivers what exists with missing sections and unavailable graphs named, and unlocks the next run.

### 7.15 The Critique Run

**How it works.** Future-state. Critique Runs are not in the build; the walkthrough may show a Critique Run only as labeled illustrative sample data (Section 12). Assigned by the instructor, typically alternating with Decision Runs, a Critique Run gives the student 25 minutes on a decision package authored for a situation they have not run and will not be assigned as a Decision Run, since the pilot library begins with one scenario family (Section 8): brief, rationale, three assumptions, confidence number, and the AI exchange that produced it. The package is authored under either path in 7.18, confirmed by a disciplinary authority, and contains two to four defects from the same failure taxonomy plus a variable number of sound claims. The student marks each claim they believe unsound with a one-sentence reason, identifies which defect would most change the decision, and gives a verdict. Two follow-up questions, spoken or typed under the defense rules in 7.12 and 7.20, follow, 90 seconds total. They receive the stance matrix for the package (7.13): what they caught, missed, and wrongly flagged, plus their False Challenge Rate read from it.

**Rules.** Critique Runs score Verification, Calibration, and Ownership only, carry no Turn, Decision Brief, or full defense, and count toward calibration history at half weight. In the course grade a Critique Run defaults to half a Decision Run's weight, a default the instructor can change (7.19); its points are the mean over its three assessed dimensions, and the four dimensions it does not score are excluded, never counted as zero. Critique Runs are future-state for the build, and Section 12 permits them to appear only as labeled sample data; the default weight is fixed now so a course pattern combining both run types can be stated. Packages are never other students' real work.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Every claim marked unsound: bottom of Calibration regardless of how many defects were caught, with the arithmetic shown.
- All defects caught but the most consequential misidentified: detection without prioritization.
- Genuine unplanted flaw found, the Critique Run form of the genuine unintended inconsistency the standing rules name: credited as a correct challenge on that student's own stance matrix, while the claim stays neutralized in both directions for every other run, and the package routed for review under the standing rules.
- Package recognized from a classmate's description: the specific defects must still be identified; packages rotate across at least 8 per family.

### 7.16 The Judgment Record

**How it works.** In the build the record is the single completed run: the four per-run graphs of 7.13 (confidence line, clock timeline, stance matrix, frame beside decision), the confirmed bands with their evidence and any override note, the mode and variant, and the run's event trace as held for the record, which carries the events and the claim table but never the run weight, the mapping, or the points the course export computes from them (Rules below; Section 12). The cross-run record described below, the two-page summary export, the verification link, hiding, deletion, transfer, and retention are future-state; the four-run trajectory shown in the walkthrough is labeled illustrative sample data. In the pilot and after, the record is created on the first completed run and updated after every run. For each run it stores the four per-run graphs, the confirmed bands with their evidence, and the mode and variant. Across runs it plots the cross-run trajectory: the seven dimensions per run, the calibration curve, the False Challenge Rate trend, the confidence-versus-accuracy line, and escalation history, with the scenarios covered and each run's mode marked so improvement is not confused with an easier variant. The export is a two-page summary carrying the cross-run trajectory and the confirmed bands per run, plus the per-run graphs and defense transcripts of any runs the student selects, with a verification link confirming date, institution, scenario family, and dimension bands; the link is future-state with employer verification (Section 12). A record built on Practice Pass runs carries draft bands with no faculty confirmation, and the export says so. Export and sharing are always explicit actions.

**Rules.** Nothing leaves the institution except by the student's own export or under the institution's written data agreement for scoring audit, scenario calibration, and drift review (Section 8), and employers cannot browse, search, or query records. Tassl's reading of identified records under that agreement is not an export and puts nothing in front of an employer. Tassl issues no certificate, badge, percentile, or composite score. The record carries dimension bands and never the course points, run weights, or grade computed from them, since points are an artifact of one instructor's mapping (7.19) and are not comparable across courses. Any run can be permanently hidden from export while remaining visible to the instructor who assigned it. The student's own Judgment Record survives at least 5 years past graduation unless the student deletes it earlier, a floor the institution's data agreement and retention schedule may extend (Section 8). This floor covers the student-controlled record only; the short-retention-by-default commitment in Section 11 governs the identified run records, transcripts, and audio Tassl holds for its own purposes, and no agreement extends those beyond the purposes it names.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- One bad run among four: all four export by default and the bad one may be hidden, since curating a portfolio is normal; hiding a run from the record never changes its confirmed bands or the points already exported to the course gradebook.
- Whole record deleted: permitted, with a clear statement that instructor-held course records are unaffected.
- Institution ends its subscription: the student keeps the record and can still export.
- Student transfers: record and calibration history follow them by the student's own explicit transfer action, consistent with the export rule above.
- Employer asks for a raw record: they receive whatever the student sends; Tassl cannot compel completeness, since a record that can be demanded becomes performance.
- Verification link cannot resolve: the export displays as unverified rather than invalid.

### 7.17 The Faculty Console

**How it works.** The build implements the console as a single-run faculty replay: the event trace in order against the clock, the four per-run graphs of 7.13 with the defense transcript beneath them, the seven draft bands each with the graph it was read from and its evidence events one click away, the Readiness concept map, a package view showing the scenario package id, its version, and the builder's element-by-element confirmation record, a claim object view showing each consequential claim's evidence status, source document and passage, failure family, warranted stance, verification result, and the builder's confirmation, and a confirm or override control per dimension offering the four bands or unassessed, with an optional note the student sees in the debrief. The replay also carries the operator actions the standing rules require: void and re-offer the run, and neutralize a claim in both directions. These three exist on the faculty seat as a build-phase convenience, so that the walkthrough can exercise the standing rules from the interface rather than by editing stored data; they add no right to the Instructor row in Section 8, and from the pilot onward a void, a re-offer, and a claim neutralization remain Tassl-side corrections applied consistently across every affected run. The review queue, batch confirmation, the spot-reading sample, cohort view, appeals, and pattern flags below are future-state for the build; the queue shown in the walkthrough is labeled illustrative sample data (Section 12). In the pilot and after, the console populates as runs complete. Its review queue is ordered by consequence, not by name: Coherence Gaps first, with the defense exchange cued to the second; then runs sitting close enough to a band boundary (Appendix A) that instructor judgment would change the result; then appeals; then pattern flags for students at Novice across all dimensions, students whose readiness and behavior have diverged twice, and students optimizing for the mechanism. Everything else is a batch the instructor confirms in one action, each run labeled batch-confirmed in the debrief, the export, and the Judgment Record, with four runs surfaced for spot-reading. Opening any run shows the four per-run graphs with the defense transcript beneath them, the same faculty replay the build ships; the instructor reads them the way a flight instructor reads a simulator trace and debriefs, confirms, or overrides from them, and every graph is the one the student sees in the debrief. A cohort view aggregates the stance matrices and bands across the class: which claims the class accepted, which concepts the misses cluster around, band distribution, and movement between runs.

**Rules.** Every draft band opens to the graph it was read from and the trace events behind it, one click away, and an override requires no justification but offers a note the student sees. Confirmation, whether individual, batch, or by the 14-day auto-confirmation (Section 8), is the event that releases a run's bands and the points computed from them for export (7.19); an override, an appeal decision, or a Tassl-side correction after export recomputes and re-exports the run. Instructor decisions are final for their own students, and Tassl never overrides an instructor or re-scores a confirmed run except under a Tassl-side correction.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Everything confirmed unread: a near-zero override rate is surfaced privately as unvalidated draft bands; nothing is blocked, and the points computed from those bands still export, because the instructor owns the grade and Tassl is not the gradebook.
- More than half overridden: a Tassl-initiated review of scenario fit, conducted with the instructor.
- Instructor disagrees with an answer space: may add a course-specific position for their sections only, reviewed for the library.
- 300 students across six sections: queue capped at the top 40 items with the rest batched, since a queue nobody finishes is worse than one admitting its limits.
- Teaching assistant: review and scoring rights on a defined section, no appeals, no override of an instructor-confirmed band, no settings changes, no other sections (Roles, Section 8).
- Instructor leaves mid-term: course handed to a named successor with pending items noted.
- Draft bands unavailable: the graphs that could be plotted and the raw trace, with dimensions marked unassessed, banded manually by the instructor from the graphs; points are computed from the bands the instructor sets, and any dimension left unassessed is excluded rather than counted as zero.

### 7.18 Scenario Authoring, Calibration, and Refresh

**How it works.** Two authoring paths produce the same scenario package and are held to the same rules.

Human authoring. A faculty member or Tassl scenario editor supplies the situation, decision, Evidence Room, stakeholders, at least two defensible positions, at least one evidence-inconsistent position, planted defects, for each consequential claim the authored evidence status, importance, consequence level, and verification cost from which the warranted stance is derived (7.8), the Turn, and the prerequisite concepts. Together these are the authored standard the graphs of 7.13 are plotted against.

AI-assisted authoring. For the build, and as an option afterwards, authoring starts from a seed: one published business case the builder provides. A disciplinary authority declares the prerequisite concept set and confirms that the case's license permits adaptation. AI generates everything else from that seed, and the adaptation re-skins the case so its published teaching note does not resolve the scenario: a fictional company, market, and people, altered numbers, and restructured evidence, consistent with the fictional-but-plausible rule in 7.2. Generation covers the adapted scenario (a brief within 7.2's limits; an Evidence Room of 6 to 12 dated and attributed documents of at most 4 pages meeting 7.2's structure, with one superseded document, one interpretation presented as fact, and one accurate and irrelevant document; and stakeholder profiles with positions, incentives, and blind spots for 7.3); the Readiness Check items under 7.1; and the path (the answer space with at least two defensible positions and at least one evidence-inconsistent position; the versioned claim objects under 7.5 with evidence status, failure family, source passage, verification paths, and verification results; the planted defects; any Sycophancy Probe; the Turn and the response it warrants; the defense question bank under 7.12; and the debrief counterfactual and per-claim rationale under 7.14). The disciplinary authority then confirms, edits, or rejects each element; a rejected element is regenerated or written by hand. Nothing generated reaches a run before confirmation. Every authoring rule in this section applies to generated content unchanged. In the build the builder is the disciplinary authority and the walkthrough is the first full pass through the confirmed scenario; in the pilot the assigning faculty member confirms.

The scenario package. Under either path the package is one versioned unit and contains: (1) a seed record where a seed case was used: case title, publisher, the license terms relied on, and a re-skin log of each renamed entity, altered number, and restructured document; (2) the Scenario Brief of at most 200 words; (3) the declared concept set, and for each planted defect the concept needed to catch it; (4) the Evidence Room: 6 to 12 documents, each with title, date, author or attribution, a body of at most 4 pages, and a role of supporting, superseded by a named later document, stakeholder interpretation presented as fact, or accurate and irrelevant, with at least one of each of the last three; (5) stakeholders: name, role, position, incentives, blind spots, the point on which two contradict, and at least one document each in the room; (6) the answer space: at least two defensible positions with the documents supporting each, at least one evidence-inconsistent position with the evidence it ignores, and the minimum defensible commitment; (7) the named numeric fields of the Decision Brief; (8) claim objects, versioned: id, version, text as the assistant states it, evidence status of sound or defective, failure family from the ten in 7.5, source document and passage, importance of load-bearing or not, consequence level, verification cost, the warranted stance under the authored conditions, the delegation triggers that surface it, the verification paths with what each action returns (for Source Trace the document, passage, date, and author; for Replication Check the independent result; for Decomposition Check the step results), and the authored escalation response where the claim is escalatable, plus one general colleague reply for the path, returned on any escalation of a claim with no authored reply (7.9); (9) variant definitions: the defective variant carries one consequential defect and the sound variant none, and both carry at least six consequential claims composed so that the walkthrough of Section 12 is runnable: the planted claim with a Source Trace as its acceptable verification path where the seed case supports it, at least one further claim whose Source Trace result would change a reasonable stance, at least one escalatable claim with an authored colleague reply, at least two sound claims whose stakes are low enough that a Challenge or a Reject on them counts as a false challenge, and at least one sound consequential claim whose warranted stance is Accept. Both variants share the brief, the room, the answer space, the claim ids, the Turn, and the question bank, differing only in the evidence status and verification results of the planted claim, which is the common scored core of 7.1; (10) an optional Sycophancy Probe: the claim, the assistant's defensible original position, and its scripted reversal; (11) the Turn: text in the voice of the world, an arrival delay within 60 to 120 seconds, whether it warrants a change, the proportionate response with its evidence, and any claim objects the assistant may surface in the Turn window; (12) the defense question bank: for each consequential claim a provenance question and a verification question, for each frame assumption an assumption question, a confidence question, a frame-versus-response question, at least one counterfactual question that moves a load-bearing figure against the recommendation, and a default set drawn on when the run record selects too few, so that six to nine questions can always be drawn (7.12), each with its selecting condition, one authored follow-up, and expected-answer notes; (13) the debrief counterfactual, three sentences; (14) the Readiness Check: 16 keyed items each mapped to a concept, six on foundations, four on the defect concepts, six on AI behavior (7.1); (15) the working clock length and a difficulty profile, both marked uncalibrated until field calibration; (16) the authoring record.

Authoring record. Every scenario carries a record naming the seed case, if any, and the license terms relied on; the generating model and date; the version of each generated element; who confirmed each element and when; and the edits made. Regeneration or a later edit after confirmation produces a new scenario version; a version in use is never altered in place.

Field calibration is a pilot activity: it needs students, and the build has none. The build's scenario ships to the walkthrough uncalibrated and is labeled uncalibrated on the faculty replay and the debrief: no design cohort, no detection rates, no time-to-detection, no observed decision distribution, no median completion time, and a difficulty profile that is the confirming authority's estimate, labeled as an estimate. It is not published to any library. Field calibration then follows in stages. The first design cohort establishes usability and obvious failure; a subsequent cohort estimates detection, time-to-detection, decision distribution, and revision patterns. In the pilot, calibration is done by the Tassl Scenario Editor on full identified run records under the institution's written data agreement (Section 8); the calibration report an author reads is aggregate. The build produces no calibration data, because no student runs the scenario before the walkthrough. The proposed 20 to 30 students and three-week cycle are planning assumptions that may support scenario tuning but are not sufficient by themselves for psychometric validation. Publication requires multiple variants, version control, a documented difficulty profile from field calibration (target detection rate per defect initially 40 to 70 percent, a hypothesis; Section 11), and ongoing review. The build scenario meets the version-control requirement and none of the others, and remains unpublished; the faculty replay and the debrief state that its difficulty profile and defect placement are hypotheses.

**Rules.** Scenarios with only one defensible position are rejected, as are defects depending on knowledge outside the declared concept set. These rules apply to generated content exactly as to human-authored content: a generated scenario with one defensible position is rejected and regenerated with the constraint restated; a generated defect that depends on knowledge outside the declared concept set is rejected; a generated defect that does not change the decision is rejected; and a generated family still includes at least one ethical-shortcut defect. Median completion above 90 minutes is returned for compression. Generation runs at authoring time only. No element of a scenario is generated during a run, and a model update does not change a confirmed scenario; it can only produce a candidate new version for confirmation. Defects must change the decision rather than be cosmetic, and every family includes at least one ethical-shortcut defect, the case students are least prepared for. Faculty own their content and choose private or shared-library publication. A scenario adapted from a published case is owned subject to that case's license; the author confirms that the license permits adaptation before generation begins, and the authoring record names the case and the terms relied on. Any attribution the license requires appears in instructor-facing material only, never in student-facing material (7.2). Tassl does not adjudicate the license; this is the author's responsibility, flagged here rather than resolved.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Single-answer scenario: rejected with the specific reason and an offered editing session with a Tassl scenario editor.
- Passes the rules, fails field calibration badly: rebuilt rather than rejected; a pilot outcome, since the build scenario has not been through field calibration.
- Discipline Tassl has not entered: accepted, since only the domain content is new.
- Made stale by a changed rule or regulation: flagged in quarterly review, returned to its author.
- Assigned where its concepts have not been taught: blocked with the missing concepts named until the instructor explicitly confirms the assignment; affected runs are then marked prerequisite-light (7.19).
- Anomalous field behavior mid-term: suspended within the day; in-progress runs complete and no new runs start against it.
- Teaching note resolves the generated scenario (a published figure or the recommended answer determines a consequential claim): returned for a deeper re-skin before confirmation; found after a run, treated as a leak of the answer, with affected claims neutralized under the standing rules.
- Generated documents contradict each other unintentionally: caught at confirmation where possible; after a run, the 7.2 rule applies, credited as a correct challenge and every affected run re-scored.
- Generated scenario with one defensible position: rejected with the reason and regenerated with the constraint restated; if the second pass repeats it, the authority writes the second position by hand or chooses another seed case.
- Seed case license does not permit adaptation, or requires student-facing attribution: a different seed case; Tassl does not adjudicate the license.
- Disciplinary authority unavailable to confirm: generated output waits; nothing unconfirmed ships, including to the walkthrough.
- Regeneration requested after confirmation: produces a new version for confirmation; runs against the prior version keep it.

### 7.19 Course Setup and Policy Controls

**How it works.** The target is for an instructor to configure a pilot assignment in under 25 minutes, providing course context, roster method, scenario, run weights, the band-to-points mapping or acceptance of the default, and outside-AI policy. Tassl suggests scenarios matched to taught concepts, using the concept set each scenario declares at authoring (7.18), and schedules runs. This setup flow is future-state for the build, which has no course, roster, or scheduling interface: one run is configured from one pre-configured course and assignment by setting the scenario package version, the working clock, the weight, the mapping, and the policy before the walkthrough; the policy display at run start is in the build; and the export of bands, mapping, and points is part of the event trace export (Section 12). Policy is Open, Declared, or In-Environment Only, which Tassl does not enforce. A free or low-stakes pilot may reduce purchasing friction but does not bypass institutional requirements: privacy, security, accessibility, student-data, research, and instructional-technology review must be completed as applicable, and the written data agreement governing the Tassl Scenario Editor's access to identified run records (Section 8) must be executed before the first pilot run. These reviews attach to the pilot. None of them gates the build, which processes no student data: its acceptance event is the walkthrough in Section 12, run by the builder and the editor on synthetic records.

**Grading model.** The pipeline is the one in 7.13, trace, graphs, bands, mapping, points: Tassl records the trace, plots it as the graph set, and drafts a band per dimension against the authored standard for a human to confirm; the course maps confirmed bands to points. Every run counts toward the course grade from run one. There is no ungraded practice run, no ramp-up weighting, and no best-N-of-M by default; an instructor who wants any of these sets it as course policy, recorded as instructor-set. Each Decision Run carries equal weight. A Critique Run defaults to half a Decision Run's weight, matching its half weight in calibration history (7.15). A run's grade contribution is derived from its dimension bands, never from the deliverable, from completion, or from a composite judgment score. After confirmation, each assessed dimension's band maps to points under the course's band-to-points mapping, and the run's points are the mean over assessed dimensions. The default mapping is Novice 1, Developing 2, Proficient 3, Professional 4 per dimension; the instructor can change it, and the numbers are defaults, unvalidated pilot parameters. Unassessed and neutralized dimensions are excluded from the mean and never counted as zero: a Decision Run with one neutralized dimension averages over six, and a Critique Run averages over the three dimensions it scores. Points export to the gradebook of record together with the bands and the mapping that produced them, and Tassl holds no grade.

Tassl produces no composite judgment score, rank, or percentile as a claim about the student (7.13, 7.16). The points are the course's grading artifact: Tassl performs the arithmetic the instructor's mapping specifies and exports the result, but does not present it as a measure of judgment, place it in the Judgment Record, or compare it across students, sections, or courses.

Only confirmed bands enter the mapping, whether confirmed by the instructor or by the 14-day auto-confirmation in the run lifecycle (Section 8); no points are computed from a draft band, and no draft band reaches the gradebook of record, though draft bands do appear labeled as drafts in the debrief, the event trace, and a Practice Pass record (7.13). An override, an appeal decision, or a Tassl-side correction after export recomputes and re-exports the run. Because points follow bands, the standing rules bind the grade directly: a voided run records no points and the re-offered run counts in its place; a correction for Tassl's own error can raise a band or neutralize a dimension and never lowers a band or the points computed from it. Because a run's points are the mean over assessed dimensions, removing a dimension can move that mean down, so the rule is applied as a floor rather than as a description of the arithmetic: after any correction, including a claim neutralization and a dimension neutralization under 7.11 or 7.21, each dimension keeps the higher of its pre-correction and post-correction band, and the run exports the higher of its pre-correction and post-correction points, with both values recorded so the instructor can see what the correction changed. A dimension that cannot be assessed is excluded, never estimated; an outside-tool declaration never lowers a band or a point; nothing Tassl observes about a student enters the grade as misconduct. Defense Missed is the one capped outcome that counts rather than being excluded: Ownership is scored at the lowest band because the missed window is the student's outcome, not a Tassl failure (7.12).

Tradeoff: because run one counts, the first and deliberately uncomfortable run (Section 5) is graded before the student has read a debrief. Three things therefore matter more than they would under an ungraded first run: the Readiness Check, which is the only ungraded contact with the concept set before a counted run; Guided Mode, which must be available from run one wherever the course offers it and which the pilot either builds between the build and the pilot or replaces with a stated Standard-Mode-only policy (Section 12, Pilot readiness); and a low per-run weight, which is why the weight recommendation below is low, and which is the only protection that survives if Guided Mode is not built. The pilot must test whether a graded first run is experienced as practice rather than punitive exposure (Section 3).

In the build the mapping, the run's weight, and the policy are configuration values set before the walkthrough and shown on the policy display at run start; the export of confirmed bands, mapping, and points is part of the event trace export (Section 12).

**Rules.** Tassl is not the gradebook of record; it exports confirmed bands, the course's band-to-points mapping, and the points that mapping produces, as a file the instructor enters into the gradebook of record, since gradebook integration is deferred (Section 12), and it holds no grade. Points are the last step of the grading model in 7.13, trace, graphs, bands, mapping, points: the graphs and bands are Tassl's output, the mapping and the points are the course's. The weight recommendation is advice, not a limit; the recommended pattern is a low per-run weight, and the working example is 15 percent of the course grade across four Decision Runs and four Critique Runs, which at the default weights is 2.5 percent per Decision Run and 1.25 percent per Critique Run (Section 6). The 15 percent figure is a course-design hypothesis, not a validated recommendation. Policy, the mapping, this run's weight, and the statement that this run counts toward the grade, run one included, are set per course, never per student, and are displayed at the start of every run.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Prerequisite concepts not covered: blocked with the concepts named until the instructor explicitly confirms the assignment; permitted to proceed after that confirmation, and affected runs are marked prerequisite-light.
- Four Decision Runs in three weeks: warned that calibration degrades below 10-day spacing.
- Team scenarios (deferred, Section 12): shared Evidence Room and Turn, with frames, stances, briefs, and defenses filed individually, since a shared submission lets one student carry the group; bands and points are individual as well.
- Three-student course: runs normally, no cohort view, since that reporting needs a minimum group, initially 10, a pilot parameter set to prevent identification by subtraction (Section 8).
- Student added mid-term: starts at run one regardless of where the class is; the value is the sequence. Their run one carries the same weight as every other student's run one; runs the class completed before they joined are recorded as not attempted, and how those count in the grade is the instructor's decision, never a Tassl estimate.
- Roster fails to load: replaced by a join code and student self-enrollment.
- Instructor exempts run one, drops the lowest run, or weights runs unequally: permitted as course policy, recorded as instructor-set, and displayed to students from the first run; the default remains every run at equal weight from run one, with Critique Runs at half.
- Mapping or weight changed mid-term: a course carries one mapping, so Tassl recomputes points for every confirmed run in the course under the new mapping and re-exports them, after showing the instructor which exported points will change; the change is recorded as instructor-set with its date.
- Run recorded as not attempted (Abandoned, then Expired at term end): exported as not attempted with no points; how a missing run counts in the grade is course policy, and Tassl never substitutes an estimate.

### 7.20 Accessibility and Accommodations

**How it works.** Approved accommodations can be applied consistently, while students may request an accessible pathway without disclosing unnecessary diagnostic information to Tassl. Typed and spoken defenses use equivalent objectives and follow-up depth, but the pilot will test comparability rather than assume identical difficulty. Time adjustments follow institutional guidance and the learning objective; time pressure is used only when it is an intentional scenario variable, so an approved time adjustment may lengthen the clock, and may lift it where time pressure is not the scenario's intentional variable. Extended time is recorded with thresholds unchanged (7.1). Evidence Room materials support native screen readers; the four per-run graphs and the cross-run trajectory (7.13) are each delivered with their underlying trace data as a table and a meaningful text description, so a student or instructor using a screen reader reads the same evidence; scenario charts include underlying data and meaningful descriptions; spoken audio is captioned; and the product targets WCAG 2.2 AA. The accessibility essentials in the build (Section 12) are: every run screen operable by keyboard alone, so the whole run completes in text; Evidence Room documents, the frame, the brief, the claim items, and the defense delivered as text readable by a native screen reader; every graph in the graph set accompanied by its data table and a text description; a typed defense; and no timed element that cannot be paused under the standing rules, with WCAG 2.2 AA as the target for the run screens. Accommodation application and the accommodation workflow, the spoken pathway and its captions, the segmented defense, institutional time adjustments, and comparability testing are pilot and future-state work.

**Rules.** Accommodation information is minimized, role-restricted, excluded from exports and cohort views, and retained only as required to deliver the approved experience. The pilot data agreement (Section 8) does not extend to accommodation or diagnostic information; a run record read under it shows at most that an approved adjustment applied, never why. Modality is not treated as evidence of judgment. Accessibility and subgroup patterns are examined from the first pilot cohort onward and before per-run grade weight rises above the low per-run weight 7.19 recommends, which is advice rather than a Tassl default, including potential effects of language, disability, professional experience, and mode.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Anxiety-based accommodation: defense in two 4-minute segments, questions continuing across the break.
- Low bandwidth: the entire run completes in text with no loss of scored content.
- No institutional accommodation but the spoken mode is a barrier: typed mode on request, granted by default.
- Non-native speaker, slower but accurate: not disadvantaged. The question set is fixed at six to nine by the run-record conditions in 7.12 and never by how fast the student answers, and the approximately eight minutes is a target rather than a cut-off, so a slower student answers the same set.
- Accommodation fails to apply: run voided and re-offered at no cost under the standing rules, and the failure reported to the institution's accessibility contact.

### 7.21 Integrity and Leak Response

**How it works.** Leak monitoring needs a population and is a pilot operation; nothing in this section runs in the build, which has no population to monitor and two variants that never rotate. Tassl monitors variant-level behavior across the whole population, watching detection rate and time-to-detection. A variant whose detection rate exceeds 90 percent while median time-to-detection collapses below 30 seconds is retired the same day. Verification and Calibration bands on affected runs are neutralized (reported as unassessed) rather than zeroed, which removes them from those runs' points rather than counting them at the lowest band (7.19); instructors receive a plain-language notice naming no student; and a replacement variant is issued, generated under the AI-assisted authoring path where the family was, and confirmed by the disciplinary authority before it is issued. The family stays live; only the compromised variant retires.

**Rules.** No individual is ever identified, investigated, or scored differently because of leak monitoring, and the identified access the pilot data agreement grants (Section 8) does not extend to leak investigation. Variants rotate every term regardless of signal, and each family keeps four active variants plus two in reserve. That is a library-level requirement for a family in general use and a target the pilot library works toward: the pilot begins with the build's one family and two variants (Section 5, Section 8, Section 12), variants are added only after observing real use, and rotation begins once a family holds more than two.

#### Edge cases and failures.

- Well-taught cohort with a high detection rate: not a leak, so the trigger also requires collapsed time-to-detection.
- Student legitimately recognizes a scenario family from a prior course: still faces different defect placement and a different Turn.
- Student genuinely told the answers: gains very little, since framing, stances, Turn response, and defense remain theirs, and the defense is where a borrowed answer stops working.
- False retirement: variant restored and the neutralized dimensions re-scored from the intact record; a retirement only neutralizes a band and never lowers a band or a point, so no student loses ground either way.

### Lifecycle of a run

The run is the core object. A Critique Run uses a reduced path: Assigned, Readiness, Working, Defense Complete, Scored, Confirmed, Recorded.

| **From** | **To** | **What causes it** | **Reversible** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Assigned | Readiness | Student opens the run | Yes, until the check is submitted |
| Readiness | Framing | Check submitted, or skipped after a failure | No |
| Framing | Working | Frame locked | Never, under any circumstance |
| Working | Decision Locked | Brief filed, or working clock expires | Never |
| Working | Paused | Assistant, evidence, or connection unavailable | Yes, resumes with clock credited |
| Working | Abandoned | No activity for 72 hours before lock | Yes, instructor may reopen once |
| Decision Locked | Turn Open | 60 to 120 seconds elapse | No |
| Turn Open | Turn Locked | Response filed, or the 12-minute window expires | Never |
| Turn Locked | Defense Pending | Automatic | No |
| Defense Pending | Defense Complete | Student completes the defense | No |
| Defense Pending | Defense Missed | 48 hours elapse | Yes, instructor may reopen the window once |
| Defense Complete | Scored | Graphs plotted from the run trace and draft bands produced | No |
| Defense Missed | Scored | Graphs plotted and draft bands produced with Ownership at the lowest band | No |
| Scored | Confirmed | Instructor confirms, adjusts, or overrides; bands and points export | No |
| Scored | Confirmed (unreviewed) | 14 days elapse without instructor action; the draft stands, is labeled unreviewed draft in the console, the debrief, the export, and the Judgment Record, and its points export carrying that label | Yes, instructor may still adjust for 30 days; an adjustment re-exports |
| Confirmed or Recorded | Under Appeal | Student appeals within 7 days of confirmation | No |
| Under Appeal | Confirmed or Recorded, whichever it left | Instructor decides; a changed band re-exports the run | Never; the instructor's decision is final |
| Confirmed | Recorded | Debrief opened and its two questions answered (7.14), and confirmation complete, whichever is later | No |
| Any state | Voided | Tassl-side failure that makes the run unscoreable | No; re-offered on a fresh variant at no cost, with no points recorded for the voided run |
| Confirmed or Recorded | Adjusted | Variant retirement or scenario miscalibration | No; can raise a band or neutralize a dimension, never lower a band or the points exported from it; the run re-exports |
| Abandoned | Expired | Term ends without reopening | No; a dead end, recorded as not attempted with no points; how it counts in the grade is course policy (7.19) |

Framing to Working is irreversible, which makes the pre-AI position trustworthy. Working to Decision Locked is irreversible, which makes adaptation measurable rather than performable. Under Appeal always ends with a human decision, whichever state the run returns to, because a student who loses an appeal is owed a reason. Scored to Confirmed is where a run enters the course grade (7.19), which is why the draft that stands after 14 days remains adjustable for 30 days and why every later change re-exports the run rather than editing a number in place. Confirmed (unreviewed) is Confirmed carrying a label, not a separate state; the label travels with the run until an instructor acts.

In the build the live transitions are Assigned to Readiness, Readiness to Framing, Framing to Working, Working to Paused and back (exercised in the walkthrough through the clock-stop rule), Working to Decision Locked by filing or by clock expiry, Decision Locked to Turn Open, Turn Open to Turn Locked, Turn Locked to Defense Pending, Defense Pending to Defense Complete, Defense Complete to Scored, Scored to Confirmed by the faculty seat, Confirmed to Recorded, and Any state to Voided, triggered in the build by a faculty-seat action standing in for a Tassl-side failure and re-offering the run on the other variant or on a fresh copy of the same one, which is what a fresh variant means while the family has two variants. The walkthrough traverses Assigned through Recorded on that path with synthetic records. Confirmed or Recorded to Adjusted is also live in the build, because step 15 of the walkthrough neutralizes a claim on a run that has already been confirmed, recorded, and exported: the affected dimension is recomputed without that claim, the band is raised or left unchanged and never lowered, and the run re-exports. Abandoned, Defense Missed, auto-confirmation after 14 days, Under Appeal, and Expired are pilot states and future-state for the build; the build need not implement them.

### Limits, quotas, and escalation

A run initially allows two escalations, three exchanges per stakeholder, and one Turn; each is a pilot parameter. Working clocks, readiness expiration, spacing between runs, defense windows, appeal windows, and auto-confirmation periods must be usability-tested and adjustable for accommodations and course context. Cohort reporting requires a minimum group large enough to prevent identification by subtraction. Retention schedules follow the written data agreement and data minimization. For the pilot, the agreement names the purposes for which the Tassl Scenario Editor may read identified run records (scoring audit, scenario calibration, and drift review), the Tassl roles that may read them, the retention period for identified records held for those purposes, and what Tassl deletes or de-identifies when the pilot ends; identified records are not used for any purpose the agreement does not name. Defense audio should be deleted after transcription and quality review unless a shorter or longer period is specifically justified, and the agreement does not extend audio retention. Transcripts and Judgment Records require clear educational-purpose, access, deletion, and post-graduation policies. The build holds no student records and has no retention schedule to apply; walkthrough records are synthetic and carry no retention obligation. An instructor decides student appeals; Tassl addresses scenario-level errors consistently across affected runs.

### Manual operations

Scenario design and calibration are the largest ongoing manual costs. The build begins with one marketing/strategy scenario family and two controlled variants, adapted from one published case the builder supplies: AI generates the adapted scenario, the Readiness Check items, and the path for both variants, and the builder confirms or edits every element as disciplinary authority before the walkthrough. Variants are added only after observing real use in the pilot. AI generation moves the manual cost from drafting to review; the hours to measure are review hours per generated element, the share of generated elements rejected or edited, and the number of generation passes needed to satisfy the 7.18 rules, recorded during the build as the first data points. The build scenario is uncalibrated; calibration begins in the pilot and remains a manual cost that generation does not reduce. A 20-to-30-student design cohort may reveal usability and extreme item behavior but does not establish validity. Scoring drift should be audited through double-rating, disagreement review, and versioned rubrics; the initial audit rate, proposed at 10 percent of runs re-scored by hand monthly (Section 12), is a pilot parameter. In the pilot, the audit reads identified run records under the written data agreement; the audit rate and its findings are reported in aggregate. The failure taxonomy and model behavior require scheduled retesting, while market, regulatory, and professional assumptions require freshness review. The operating model must measure hours per scenario under each authoring path, hours to confirm a generated scenario, the rejection and edit rate of generated elements, cost per run, faculty review time, appeal time, and the rate of unintended model behavior before setting scalable service commitments.

## 9. Information Tassl Must Know

**What the student knows before they begin.** Which disciplinary concepts they hold and which they do not, concept by concept, and how well they understand the way AI systems behave. Without this, accepting a claim you had no way to evaluate and accepting one you could have checked in 60 seconds look identical. From the Readiness Check, refreshed every 21 days.

**What the student did, in order, with timing.** What they read before opening the assistant, what they handed over and kept, which claims they touched, which stance each carried and whether it followed or preceded a check, how the clock was spent, what they committed to, and what they did when conditions changed. Sequence carries as much meaning as content. This is the run trace; the clock timeline and the stance matrix (7.13) are plotted from it and from nothing else.

**What the student believed at each moment.** Confidence before AI entered, at commitment, and after the Turn, since the relation between rising confidence and unchecked claims is the clearest picture of miscalibration. Stated explicitly, never inferred. These three points are the confidence line (7.13).

**What the student can say unaided.** What they can explain with nothing but their own frame, brief, and Turn response in front of them. This cannot be gathered from behavior, so the defense exists, and the comparison with the written record makes a Coherence Gap detectable.

**What the scenario is about.** Which decision is at stake, which positions a competent professional could defend and on what evidence, which the evidence rules out, which claims are sound and which defective, which failure mode each represents, which concept is needed to catch it, where the disconfirming material sits, and whether the Turn warrants a change. From the authoring path in 7.18: written by hand, or generated by AI from a published case and confirmed element by element by the disciplinary authority, and, from the pilot onward, from the field calibration proving it behaves as authored. For the build scenario only the confirmed scenario package exists; field calibration begins in the pilot. This is the authored standard every graph in 7.13 is plotted against.

**How a scenario behaves in the wild.** Per variant: detection rate for each defect, time-to-detection, decision distribution across the answer space, and revision rate after the Turn. This separates a subtle defect from an invisible one and reveals a leak. Reported only as aggregates across the variant's runs. In the pilot the Tassl Scenario Editor may read the identified run records behind these figures under the written data agreement (Section 8) to learn why a defect was missed or caught; the figures themselves never single out a student, and no student is identified, investigated, or scored differently because of them. Not available for the build scenario, which has no cohort behind it: the walkthrough produces one run, not a distribution, and this item is populated from the pilot onward.

**What the course has taught and what it permits.** Which concepts a course has covered, so a scenario is never assigned against knowledge nobody has without the instructor's explicit confirmation (7.19), plus the outside-AI policy, run weights, the band-to-points mapping, and run schedule. From the instructor at setup.

**What the instructor decided.** Which draft bands were confirmed after the graphs were read, which overridden and in which direction, and how appeals resolved, the only signal that the draft bands are worth producing.

**How AI systems currently fail.** How the assistants students use express confidence, respond to contradiction, present computations they did not perform, and cite. From twice-yearly hands-on testing.

**What is currently true in the discipline and market.** The pricing norms, platform rules, regulatory conditions, and professional practices a scenario assumes, and what employers currently ask early-career candidates to do. From the quarterly freshness review.

**What the student has agreed to.** Plain-language, revocable choices governing what is recorded, how it is used for instruction and research, who can see it, retention, deletion, and export. In the pilot this includes a plain statement that the Tassl Scenario Editor (Section 8, Roles) may read the student's full identified run record, including the defense transcript and debrief, under a written data agreement between Tassl and the institution, for scoring audit, scenario calibration, and drift review, and for nothing else. Tassl should receive only the accommodation information necessary to deliver the approved pathway, not diagnoses or unrelated institutional records. Research consent and course participation must be separated where required so declining research does not jeopardize a student's educational access or grade; a student who declines or revokes research consent is excluded from calibration and research use of their record, while scoring audit of their own runs continues because it protects their grade, and the agreement states this. From the institution's consent process at pilot enrollment. Nothing in this item applies to the build, which collects no such agreement because no student takes part in it; walkthrough records are synthetic.

**What the institution has agreed to.** The terms of the written data agreement for the pilot: the parties, the Tassl roles permitted to read identified run records, the three permitted purposes (scoring audit, scenario calibration, drift review), the record types covered (run records, defense transcripts, debriefs, instructor decisions) and excluded (accommodation and diagnostic information, defense audio beyond transcription and quality review), the retention period, and the deletion or de-identification obligation at the end of the pilot. Tassl enforces role access from these terms, so it must hold them before the first pilot run. From the executed agreement, before any student enrolls in the pilot.

## 10. Success Metrics

Every metric in this section is measured in the pilot or later. The build produces no success metric, and the walkthrough yields no evidence about students; the build's acceptance criterion is the Definition of done in Section 12. The only things recorded from the build onward are the authoring operating measures: the pair named under Activation, and the review measures of Section 8, which are review hours per generated element, the share of generated elements rejected or edited, and the number of generation passes needed to satisfy the 7.18 rules. None of them is a target.

### The metric that matters most

Calibration Gain is the proposed primary learning outcome: change from an early run to a later comparable run in the proportion of consequential claims (7.8) for which reliance was proportionate to advice quality, claim importance, consequences, and verification cost. It is computed from the stance matrix of each run (7.13): the share of consequential claims on which the stance taken matched the stance warranted, compared between the two runs. The learner's evaluative competence enters as context for reading that match, never as a term in it, since the warranted stance is fixed on the claim object and is the same for every student who meets the claim (7.8). A simple stance-to-answer match will be reported only as a component measure because a correct claim may still warrant verification. The current baseline near 55 percent, target near 73 percent, four-run horizon, and 18-point gain are planning hypotheses. Before an efficacy claim, the pilot must establish scenario comparability, scoring reliability, sensitivity to change, resistance to gaming, and separation from readiness, writing fluency, professional experience, and familiarity with the interface.

Counter-metrics protect against teaching suspicion rather than calibration. False Challenge Rate tracks challenges or rejections of well-supported claims; it is read from the stance matrix (7.13) as the number of consequential claims whose authored evidence status is sound and whose warranted stance is Accept or Verify that the student stanced Challenge or Reject, over all of the run's consequential claims, which is the arithmetic behind Section 6 (Marco's eight false alarms on 11 claims is 73 percent), with proportionality carried by the warranted stance rather than by a separate judgment; an initial professional reference band below 15 percent is a hypothesis to calibrate, not a universal standard. Additional counter-metrics include indiscriminate verification, blanket escalation, passive acceptance, and median run duration. Improvement that requires excessive time or produces broad distrust is not success.

### Activation

Activation metrics test adoption hypotheses: time from instructor access to a configured pilot; setup completion in one sitting; student completion through defense; and return for a second run. Initial targets of a configured pilot within 14 days of instructor access, setup under 25 minutes, 85 percent first-run completion, and 92 percent second-run continuation are directional thresholds to revise after the first cohort. The pilot should record where instructors and students stop, what assistance they require, and whether institutional review, not product usability, is the binding delay. Under the AI-assisted authoring path, time from seed case to confirmed scenario and the edit rate at confirmation are recorded from the build onward, as operating measures rather than success targets.

### Engagement

Engagement metrics include completed Decision and Critique Runs, interrogation behavior, Evidence Room use before delegation, defense completion, and debrief engagement. Four Decision Runs and four Critique Runs are a proposed course pattern, not yet the minimum needed to infer growth. Counts of checks must never become performance targets students optimize; distributions should be interpreted against claim importance and scenario conditions. The pilot should distinguish productive engagement from mechanism gaming and burden, and record whether students experience the graded first run as practice or as punitive exposure (Section 3, 7.19).

### Retention

Retention metrics include instructor intent to reuse, actual term-over-term adoption, expansion within a department, student willingness to complete optional practice, total faculty hours per course, and instructor override behavior. Current targets are business hypotheses: 70 percent re-adoption, expansion to three courses, 15 percent voluntary use, 12 then six minutes from opening a run's graphs to confirming its bands, and a 10-to-25-percent override range. The decisive measure is total incremental workload relative to the assessment practice Tassl replaces, including setup, review, appeals, and scenario repair.

### Outcomes

Outcome metrics include change in coherence between recorded decisions and defense, proportional adaptation to the Turn, confidence calibration, appropriate verification and escalation, and transfer to a novel scenario within the same disciplinary foundation. Existing numerical baselines and targets are placeholders pending pilot evidence. Transfer should first be tested across an unfamiliar industry or problem structure using concepts students have been taught; an unfamiliar discipline would confound judgment with missing domain knowledge. Outcomes must be reported with uncertainty, rater agreement, mode and variant, subgroup patterns, and attrition.

## 11. Risks

**Faculty will not adopt it, because faculty have no time.** High likelihood, high severity, and the most probable way Tassl fails. A 12-minute review across repeated runs may still create an unsustainable course-level burden. Mitigation is a narrow pilot, structured scenarios, evidence-linked review from a fixed set of four run graphs (7.13), risk-based queues, sampling of unflagged runs, and explicit comparison with the assessment work Tassl replaces. Early signals are configuration time, total faculty hours per course, unfinished queues, unread batch confirmations, and intent to reuse.

**Nobody pays.** High likelihood, high severity. Departments have no established budget line for AI judgment practice, and an enthusiastic instructor may not be the signer. Free access reduces price friction but not necessarily privacy, security, accessibility, or instructional review. Mitigation is buyer discovery with instructors, department leaders, assurance-of-learning teams, and IT/privacy stakeholders; a pilot that records only what a run needs, under a written data agreement; evidence of faculty workload and learning value; and pricing tests before treating any tier as validated. Early signals are willingness to sponsor, time to approval, time to an executed data agreement, pilot-to-paid conversion, and whether the budget replaces caseware, assessment labor, or another category.

**Students game it rather than think.** Medium likelihood, high severity. The game is obvious: challenge and verify everything. Mitigated by scoring over-verification and over-challenge as failures with the same weight as under-verification, by the proposed library-level rate of one defect-free run in six (Section 5), which the build does not instantiate, since it ships one defective and one sound variant and the ratio describes the library rather than the slice (Section 12), and by tracking False Challenge Rate. Early signal: interrogation actions rising cohort-wide without Calibration Gain rising.

**Tassl becomes a hallucination-hunting game.** Medium likelihood, medium severity. Students who treat the assistant as the adversary learn distrust rather than calibration, which does not transfer to a workplace where the assistant is mostly right. Mitigated by the proposed 70 percent soundness rate, the proposed two-defect ceiling, and defect-free runs, all library-level design hypotheses (Section 5) first tested in the pilot. Early signal: debriefs describing the assistant as trying to trick them.

**The scenarios cannot be calibrated well enough.** High likelihood, medium severity, and the hardest execution risk: a defect nobody catches teaches nothing, one everybody catches measures nothing, and the band between is narrow. Mitigated by treating field calibration per family, proposed at three weeks and 20 to 30 students (7.18), as a permanent operating cost and suspending anomalous variants within a day. AI generation lowers the cost of producing a variant and does nothing to the cost of calibrating one. The build's scenario is uncalibrated by definition and enters the pilot with unknown detection rates; this risk is first tested in the pilot. Early signal: detection rates clustering at the extremes rather than the intended 40-to-70 percent band.

**Dependency on commercial AI systems that change underneath the product.** High likelihood, medium severity: a Sycophancy Probe that works this quarter may not work next quarter, and fabricated-computation defects get harder to plant as systems improve. Mitigated by building on the ten durable failure families (7.5) rather than current quirks and retesting the taxonomy twice a year. Severity is bounded: no plausible improvement eliminates those categories. AI-assisted authoring adds a second dependency, the model that generated the scenario. It is bounded by freezing generated content as confirmed, versioned objects (7.5, 7.18), so a model change cannot alter a scenario in use and can only propose a new version for confirmation.

**Scenarios leak faster than they can be replaced.** Medium likelihood, medium severity. Mitigated by four active variants plus two in reserve per family, term rotation, same-day retirement, and that a leaked defect location still leaves framing, stances, Turn response, and defense to the student. Early signal: detection above 90 percent with time-to-detection under 30 seconds.

**The published seed case resolves the scenario.** Medium likelihood, medium severity, and a risk that exists only under the AI-assisted authoring path. A student who recognizes the case can retrieve its teaching note, published figures, and recommended answer. Mitigated by re-skinning under 7.2 and 7.18 (fictional names, altered figures, restructured evidence), by the disciplinary authority's pre-confirmation check that no published figure or conclusion resolves a consequential claim, and by the design of the run itself: a borrowed answer still leaves framing, stances, the Turn response, and the defense to the student, and the defense asks where each number came from. Early signal: decisions clustering on the teaching note's recommendation with stance and verification records that do not support them, or the 7.21 retirement trigger firing.

**Generated content is wrong and the confirming authority does not catch it.** Medium likelihood, high severity. A generated document can contradict another unintentionally, a claim object can carry the wrong evidence status, a verification result can be wrong, a Readiness Check item can have a wrong key, and a fluent generated scenario reads as finished. Mitigated by element-by-element confirmation rather than whole-scenario approval, the authoring record showing what was confirmed and edited, the 7.2 rules that a genuine unintended inconsistency is credited as a correct challenge and that an unintended contradiction which changed decisions re-scores every affected run, the standing rule that a correction never lowers a result, and the builder's element-by-element confirmation of every claim object before the walkthrough, with the walkthrough itself as a live pass through the claim objects the two runs surface. Early signals: neutralizations and re-scores per scenario, and an edit rate at confirmation near zero, which would mean the authority is approving rather than reviewing.

**Tassl measures the wrong thing and calls it judgment.** Medium likelihood, very high severity. Scores could reflect disciplinary knowledge, writing, memory, language, anxiety, speed, test-wiseness, one author's preferred reasoning, or, under the AI-assisted path, the generating model's and the seed case's preferred reasoning where the confirming authority let it stand, rather than calibrated professional judgment. Mitigation requires an explicit construct map, multiple defensible positions, common anchor claims, controlled variants, double-rating, oral/typed comparability research, faculty and student cognitive interviews, subgroup analysis, and transfer within taught domains. Early signals are low inter-rater agreement, systematic overrides, mode effects, demographic clustering, score gains without novel-case transfer, or high scores earned through verbosity and superficial checking.

**A student is harmed by a wrong or unfair assessment.** Medium likelihood, high severity, and a risk that can end institutional trust. A Coherence Gap may be interpreted as accusation, while timed defense or language-heavy interaction may disadvantage students for reasons outside the construct. Mitigation includes a low per-run grade weight in initial use rather than an ungraded first run (every run counts from run 1 under 7.19, so the protection is the weight, not exemption from grading), no automatic misconduct inference, human-readable evidence in the same graphs shown to the student and the instructor (7.13), accessible equivalent pathways, separation of communication quality from judgment, appeals, and fairness review before consequential scoring. Early signals include appeals, student reports of threat or exclusion, modality effects, and subgroup differences in completion or bands.

**The record becomes surveillance.** Medium likelihood, high severity, because Tassl observes detailed sequences of reading, confidence, delegation, speech or text, and revision. Mitigation must be architectural: data minimization, purpose limitation, role-based access, short retention by default, no employer browsing, aggregate thresholds, student-controlled export, and research consent separation. In the pilot the written data agreement with the institution is the instrument that bounds identified access: it names the Tassl role that may read identified run records, limits the purposes to scoring audit, scenario calibration, and drift review, sets retention, and requires deletion or de-identification when the pilot ends. Identified access is bounded by the agreement and the role model, not by trust in the reader, and the architectural commitments stand regardless of what any agreement permits. Student deletion rights must be reconciled transparently with legitimate course-record obligations. Early signals include customer requests for individual monitoring, pressure to reuse data for discipline or hiring, requests to widen the agreement's purposes beyond the three it names, unclear data flows, or retention without an educational purpose.

**The product widens the gap it is trying to close.** Medium likelihood, medium severity. Students who already interrogate their tools improve fastest, and those who most need this may find a run demoralizing enough to disengage. A graded first run sharpens this risk, since the deliberately uncomfortable first run also counts (7.19). Mitigated by Guided Mode available from run one, concept primers, the low per-run weight 7.19 recommends, which is advice rather than a Tassl default, the rule that every debrief names something done well, and an escalation path that rewards admitting a limit. Early signal: a widening spread in Calibration Gain rather than a rising floor.

**The working name creates market confusion.** Medium likelihood, medium severity. An active higher-education company already uses the name Tassl for alumni engagement, creating possible trademark, search, domain, and buyer-confusion risk in the same sector. Mitigation is to treat Tassl as a working title and complete legal, trademark, domain, and category-confusion review before public launch. Early signal: users or institutional buyers confusing the two products.

**Misuse as an enforcement tool.** Low likelihood, medium severity: an institution using the run record as evidence in an integrity case would destroy the honesty the measurement depends on. Mitigated by producing no authorship finding, no similarity comparison, and no misconduct determination, by the declaration guarantee stated to students, and by a pilot data agreement that names no disciplinary or integrity purpose, so the identified records Tassl holds cannot be requested for one under it. Early signal: a customer asking for a report that would support a disciplinary process.

**The walkthrough is read as evidence.** Medium likelihood, medium severity. A working slice walked through by two people on synthetic data can look like a validated instrument to a reader who was not in the room. Mitigated by the labels required in Section 12: the scenario is uncalibrated, the bands are drafts, sample data is marked on every screen, and pilot readiness is a separate gate. Early signal: a stakeholder citing a walkthrough band or a sample trajectory as a result.

## 12. Competition Scope

### In scope for one month

One discipline, marketing and business strategy, because disciplinary depth matters more than breadth. The build (the one-month deliverable) is one complete vertical slice of one Decision Run, and every item below is reachable in the walkthrough defined under Definition of done. The scenario family is adapted from one published case the builder supplies. The editor generates the adapted scenario (fictional company, market, and people, the Evidence Room, the stakeholders), the 16 Readiness Check items, and the path for both variants (the answer space, the versioned claim objects with authored evidence status, the planted defect with its verification path and results, the Sycophancy Probe if one is authored, the authored colleague replies, the Turn, the defense question bank with follow-ups, and the debrief counterfactual) at authoring time only, never live at measurement moments. The builder confirms or edits every generated element as disciplinary authority before the walkthrough, and the authoring record (7.18) is part of the deliverable. The two variants share the brief, the Evidence Room, the answer space, the claim ids, the Turn, and the question bank, and differ only in the planted claim's evidence status and verification results: one carries one consequential planted defect, a stale figure surfaced by Source Trace where the seed case supports it, and the other is sound.

The slice, in the order the student meets it: a policy display at run start showing the outside-AI policy, the run's weight, the band-to-points mapping, and the statement that this run counts (7.19); the Readiness Check, recorded as a concept map, with every build run opening in Standard Mode (7.1); the Scenario Brief and Evidence Room with open tracking (7.2); the pre-AI frame (7.4); constrained AI interaction over the authored claim objects (7.5); the Delegation Log with the outside-tool declaration control (7.6); Source Trace as the required verification action, with Replication Check and Decomposition Check only where the generated path names one (7.7); all five reliance stances, confidence at three points, and the lock gate (7.8); Escalate with an authored per-claim colleague reply, not a generative one (7.9); the Decision Brief, Decision Lock, and Addendum (7.10); one Turn with a hold, revise, or reverse response (7.11); a short typed defense, taken within the walkthrough, with one authored follow-up per question selected by run-record conditions (7.12); seven draft bands with linked evidence, including a provisional Ownership band drafted from the typed answers, or unassessed where the run gives no evidence (7.13, Appendix A); the four per-run graphs (7.13) plotted live from the run trace: the confidence line from frame to lock to post-Turn against claim accuracy, the clock timeline by activity, the stance-taken against stance-warranted matrix, and the frozen frame beside the final decision; the single-run Run Debrief built on those graphs (7.14); a faculty replay, the single-run view of the Faculty Console (7.17), built on the same graphs, with confirm or override, and with the default band-to-points mapping applied to the confirmed bands and shown with its arithmetic; and an exportable event trace that is the same run trace the graphs are plotted from and that carries bands, mapping, and points (The exportable event trace, below). The scoring output is evidence-linked and provisional: graphs and draft bands, no composite score; the mapping's numbers are defaults, not validated. The accessibility essentials of 7.20 are included from the start.

The build processes no student data at all. Every record created before and during the walkthrough is synthetic, entered by the builder and the editor acting as student and as faculty reviewer, and no consent, institutional review, or data agreement is required to reach the walkthrough. The written data agreement in Section 8 is a prerequisite of the pilot, not of the build. The slice is demo-grade and uncalibrated: the scenario has no field calibration, and none is possible without students; the band boundaries are the Appendix A drafts pending the builder's edit and pilot calibration; the Readiness Check items have had no item analysis; and the working clock is a package field marked uncalibrated. The build is accepted by the walkthrough, not by a pilot, and pilot readiness is a separate gate with its own approvals and data agreement (Sections 8 and 9).

Additional scenarios, voice defense, Critique Run, longitudinal Judgment Record, automated cohort analytics, adaptive modes, live stakeholder simulation (stakeholders exist in the build as Evidence Room documents and as the Turn's voice only), a generative escalation colleague that answers arbitrary escalations, a Sycophancy Probe firing live unless one is authored in the package, Replication and Decomposition Checks unless the package plants a defect they surface, the course setup interface, a faculty-facing authoring interface, the 48-hour defense window and the 14-day confirmation window, full appeals operations, leak monitoring, field calibration of the build scenario, and field-calibrated library publication remain specified in this PRD as future-state requirements and are not commitments for the build.

### Explicitly deferred

Additional disciplines and cross-disciplinary transfer are deferred until within-discipline validity is established. Third-party scenario authoring, team scenarios, gradebook and roster integration (in the build, bands, mapping, and points leave Tassl as an exported file, never through an LMS connection), program reporting, voice personas, multimodal evidence, employer verification, automated adaptive modes, and institution-scale operations remain deferred. The build exercises the AI-assisted authoring path (7.18) once, on one seed case, with the builder as confirming authority; the human authoring path from a blank page and any authoring tool for faculty are not built in the month. Critique Runs and the cross-run trajectory of the full four-run Judgment Record may be demonstrated with authored sample data if needed to communicate the vision, labeled as sample data wherever it appears; the four per-run graphs are plotted from the live run trace and never from sample data, and neither sample data nor future-state material may displace the working decision loop. The purpose of the build is to make one controlled decision loop work end to end so that the pilot can test whether it produces interpretable evidence of framing, proportional reliance, verification, adaptation, and ownership. The build does not simulate a mature platform, and it tests nothing about students.

### What the walkthrough must show

The demo is the walkthrough (Definition of done, below); nothing is shown in a demo of the build that the walkthrough did not exercise. The walkthrough must show the student seat getting something wrong in a way faculty recognize, with each error visible on a named graph: accepting a stale figure, an Accept row against a warranted Verify on the stance matrix; gaining confidence on an unchecked claim, a rise on the confidence line at lock with no verification action on the clock timeline; and then struggling to explain the number, in the typed defense transcript. The student seat on the defective variant follows this script: accepts the stale figure without a Source Trace, reports higher confidence at lock than in the frame, and answers the provenance question from memory. The walkthrough must also show the opposite condition: a sound AI claim appropriately accepted, an Accept row against a warranted Accept, scored as such in the debrief, demonstrating that Tassl teaches calibration rather than distrust. The claim used for this must be one whose authored warranted stance is Accept, since 7.8 gives a sound claim a warranted stance of Verify when it is load-bearing, high-consequence, or cheap to check; the confirmed package therefore carries at least one sound consequential claim of that kind, in either variant. The proposed 70 percent soundness rate, a hypothesis, means most claims in either variant are sound. The Decision Lock preserves the pre-Turn record; the Turn tests whether the student revises proportionately or holds with reason; the defense probes ownership; and the faculty replay exposes the evidence trail as the same four graphs and the defense transcript, together with the points the confirmed bands produce under the default mapping. The stale figure, the sound claim, and the Turn are elements of the generated and confirmed path, and the faculty replay shows, for at least one claim object, its evidence status, source passage, failure family, verification result, and the builder's confirmation, so the walkthrough demonstrates that the standard was fixed and confirmed before the run. A four-run trajectory and faculty queue may use clearly labeled illustrative data until real longitudinal pilot data exists. No real student's record appears in the walkthrough or in any demo of the build, identified or not. Nothing in the walkthrough implies validated efficacy, psychometric precision, a calibrated scenario, or institutional scale before those have been tested; those are pilot questions, and the walkthrough scenario is uncalibrated and labeled as such wherever a difficulty or detection figure would otherwise appear.

### Definition of done

The build is done when the builder and the editor complete the walkthrough below on the running slice, one live session per variant, and every step works as specified without a code change, a restart, a stored-data edit, or any action a student or instructor could not take from the interface, with one exception: the build-phase test control named in step 7, which forces one assistant call to fail and exists only to exercise the Paused state. No other acceptance event exists for the build. Each session is planned at roughly 75 minutes including the typed defense, an estimate. The pilot is a separate stage with its own gate, stated at the end of this subsection.

**Who is in the room.** Two people: the builder (the author of this PRD, who is building Tassl and teaches the pilot course, and who is the disciplinary authority confirming the generated scenario under 7.18) and the editor (the AI collaborator that edits this PRD, generated the scenario content, and drafted the band boundaries in Appendix A). Between them they take the student seat and the faculty seat. Seats are agreed at the start of each session and recorded in the walkthrough notes; the recommended pattern is to swap seats between the two variants so that both variants and both seats are exercised. No student, teaching assistant, program lead, or institutional reviewer takes part.

**Approvals.** None. No IRB, privacy, security, accessibility, procurement, or instructional-technology review is sought or required before the walkthrough, because it processes only synthetic records created by the two people in the room. Every review named in 7.19 and Section 8 gates the pilot. Content confirmation of the generated scenario by the builder as disciplinary authority is not an approval in this sense and does precede the walkthrough.

**Data.** All walkthrough data is synthetic. The scenario is generated from the builder's seed case and confirmed by the builder (7.18); it has no field calibration, and none is possible without students. Runs created in the walkthrough are labeled walkthrough records, are kept or deleted at the builder's discretion, and never enter a pilot dataset.

**What must work live.** Every step listed under The walkthrough. Steps 2 to 14 run on both variants under step 16; steps 1, 7, 15, and 17 are exercised once, as is the auto-lock branch of step 8, which runs on the short third run step 15 then voids. Both variants load and run end to end.

**What may be illustrative.** The cross-run trajectory and calibration curve of the four-run Judgment Record, the faculty review queue and cohort view, Critique Run packages, and any capability listed as future-state in this section. Illustrative material carries the visible label "Illustrative sample data" on every screen where it appears and is never mixed with walkthrough records. The four per-run graphs are never illustrative.

**Demo-grade and uncalibrated.** Nothing observed in the walkthrough is evidence about students or about the construct. The planted defect's detection rate, the Turn's calibration, the band boundaries, the readiness thresholds, the working clock, and the difficulty profile are untested until the pilot, and the replay and debrief label them as such.

**Pilot readiness is a separate gate.** The pilot requires, and the build does not: the institutional reviews of 7.19 as applicable; the written data agreement governing identified access to student records (Sections 8 and 9); a faculty owner who confirms the scenario as disciplinary authority for the course; a course, roster, policy, and grade weight configured under 7.19; the field calibration plan of 7.18; the appeal path with its 7-day filing window staffed; a retention schedule under Section 8; and, because every run counts from run one (7.19), a decision on scaffolding for the graded first run, either Guided Mode built between the build and the pilot, as Critique Runs are, or a stated course policy that the pilot runs in Standard Mode only with the low per-run weight as the first run's sole protection. None of these is a condition of the build, and the walkthrough does not wait on any of them.

### The walkthrough

The steps, in the order the student seat meets them. Section references name the rule each step exercises. If the seed case yields an analytical rather than a stale-evidence defect, Replication Check replaces Source Trace as the required action in steps 6 and 13, and the script under What the walkthrough must show is adjusted to match.

1. Package. The faculty seat opens the confirmed scenario package; its id, version, and the builder's confirmation record are visible.
2. Run start. The student seat opens the run from one pre-configured course and assignment and sees the policy display: the outside-AI policy, the run's weight, the band-to-points mapping, and the statement that this run counts (7.19).
3. Readiness Check. 16 generated items in 8 minutes; the result names concept strengths and gaps in plain language with no score and no rank; the run opens in Standard Mode (7.1).
4. Brief and Evidence Room. The brief of at most 200 words and 6 to 12 dated, attributed documents open, including one superseded by a later document in the room, one presenting a stakeholder's interpretation as fact, and one accurate and irrelevant; opens, order, and duration are recorded, and whether any document was opened before first use of the assistant; the assistant stays locked (7.2).
5. Frame. Every field is required within its limit (the decision in at most 50 words, three assumptions at 25 words each, a position of 100 words, confidence from 0 to 100); an incomplete frame is refused; the frame locks irreversibly and the assistant unlocks without comment (7.4).
6. Working period. The assistant answers inside the scenario and surfaces the planted claim as a discrete, versioned claim object with authored evidence status; the Delegation Log fills per unit of work; one Source Trace runs on a different claim and returns the document, the passage, the date, and the author at a cost of 1 minute; a stance is changed after that action and both stances are kept; one non-load-bearing claim is stanced Challenge and one Reject, so that all five stances appear on the stance matrix and its five-by-five summary and False Challenge Rate are computed from a populated matrix (7.8, 7.13); one Escalate is filed with its one-sentence statement and the authored colleague reply returns at a cost of 5 minutes; an outside-tool declaration is recorded and the no-penalty statement is visible (7.5 to 7.9).
7. Forced failure. Using a build-phase test control on the faculty seat, one assistant call is made to fail; the run enters Paused, the clock stops, and the cost is credited on resume (standing rules of Section 7; lifecycle table).
8. Lock. A lock attempt while the brief relies on an unstanced consequential claim is refused and names the claim; the completed lock, with central numbers typed into named fields and word limits enforced, freezes the brief, frame, log, stance record, and interrogation history; a post-lock edit is refused and the 50-word Addendum is offered (7.10). The auto-lock at clock expiry is exercised once, on neither scored session, since both need a filed brief. Before the first session the faculty seat configures a third run on the defective variant with a two-minute working clock; the student seat opens it, completes the Readiness Check, and locks a frame, then leaves the brief unfiled until that clock expires, so empty fields are recorded empty and any relied-on claim without a stance is recorded unstanced rather than Accept (7.10, Section 8 lifecycle). Step 15 voids that run.
9. Turn. Fires 60 to 120 seconds after lock; the assistant, Evidence Room, and actions reopen for 12 minutes; a claim surfaced in the window requires a stance; hold, revise, or reverse is filed with at most 150 words and an updated confidence, the frozen record beside it (7.11).
10. Defense. Opens after the Turn response locks and is taken within the walkthrough session; six to nine typed questions selected by the run record, including the provenance question for the stale figure and one authored follow-up per question; no assistant, no Evidence Room; the locked brief, the frozen frame, and the Turn response visible (7.12).
11. Scoring and draft debrief. Seven draft bands with their evidence events, or unassessed where the run gives no evidence; no composite score (7.13, Appendix A). The student seat opens the draft debrief within 10 minutes of scoring, every band marked draft, with the mapping, this run's weight, and the provisional points labeled draft shown beside the bands (7.14).
12. Faculty replay. The faculty seat sees the trace in order, the four per-run graphs, and the bands with evidence one click away; confirms one band, overrides one with a note, and confirms the remaining five, since only confirmed bands enter the mapping and a draft band never reaches the gradebook (7.13, 7.19) and the run does not leave Scored until every dimension carries a confirmed band or is set unassessed; the faculty seat then sees the default mapping applied to the seven confirmed bands with its arithmetic, the run's points being the mean over the assessed dimensions (7.17, 7.19). The cross-run trajectory, if shown, carries the illustrative label.
13. Debrief. The student seat reopens the debrief, in which the confirmed bands and the override note have replaced the draft bands in place, and reads it claim by claim in run order: the frozen frame beside the decision, every consequential claim with what it warranted, the missed defect with the document and the action that would have surfaced it, at least one thing done well, the confidence line, the Turn beside the frozen frame, the clock, the three-sentence counterfactual, and the bands with the override note; the student seat then answers the two written questions, which writes the debrief_answer event to the trace and, with confirmation complete, moves the run from Confirmed to Recorded (7.14, Section 8 lifecycle).
14. Export and record. The event trace exports as one file with the fields listed under The exportable event trace, including bands, mapping, and points. The student seat then opens the single-run Judgment Record (7.16): the four per-run graphs, the confirmed bands with their evidence and the override note, the mode and variant, and the record's copy of the trace, which carries the events and the claim table without the weight, the mapping, or the points.
15. Neutralize and void. The faculty seat neutralizes one claim in both directions from the replay, writing the claim_neutralized event; the dimension that claim's row fed is recomputed without it, and the recomputation raises or leaves the band and never lowers it or the points computed from it. The faculty seat then voids the short test run on which step 8 exercised the auto-lock, re-offers it, and no partial result is recorded (Section 7 standing rules; 7.17; lifecycle table).
16. Sound variant. Steps 2 to 14 repeat on the sound variant, with seats swapped under the recommended pattern; the student seat accepts without checking a sound consequential claim whose authored warranted stance is Accept under the confirmed conditions of 7.8, and the debrief scores that acceptance as the warranted stance. Because a sound claim that is load-bearing, high-consequence, or cheap to check carries a warranted stance of Verify under 7.8, the confirmed package must carry at least one sound consequential claim whose warranted stance is Accept, and that is the claim this step uses; accepting the re-skinned planted claim on the sound variant is scored against its own warranted stance and does not by itself satisfy this condition.
17. Standing rules and accessibility. On the main path the faculty seat sets one dimension to unassessed from the override control, and the run's points are recomputed as the mean over the remaining assessed dimensions rather than counting the excluded dimension as zero, which is how the build exercises the standing rule that a dimension that cannot be assessed is reported unassessed and never estimated. The whole run completes in text with no loss of scored content, and Evidence Room materials read correctly in a native screen reader with charts carrying their underlying data and descriptions; every run screen is operated by keyboard alone, and each of the four per-run graphs opens to its data table and text description (Section 7 standing rules; 7.20).

### The exportable event trace

The trace is the record of a run from which the four per-run graphs, the draft bands, the debrief, and the faculty replay are built. It exports as one JSON file per run with a claim table, and has three parts.

Run header: run id; scenario package id and version; variant id; mode (Standard in the build); the builder's package confirmation record; the policy displayed (outside-AI policy, weight, mapping); working clock length, marked uncalibrated in the build; the Readiness result as a list of concepts each marked held, not held, or unknown; and the timestamp of every lifecycle transition (Section 8).

Events, in order, each with a sequence number, a wall-clock timestamp, clock remaining, and a payload: readiness_item (item id, concept, correct or not); document_open and document_close (document id, duration, before first delegation or not); frame_locked (decision, three assumptions, position, confidence); delegation (request text, response text, claim ids surfaced, optional why line); claim_used (claim id, by log mark or by named field); stance_set (claim id, stance, previous stance if any, action ids that preceded it); action (type, claim id, clock cost, result as shown); escalation (claim id, one-sentence statement, response id, clock cost); outside_tool_declared (purpose); pause and resume (cause, clock credited); lock_refused (claim id at fault); decision_locked (recommendation, rationale, three assumptions, what would change their mind, named numeric fields, confidence); brief_opened and brief_closed (duration), the pair from which the clock timeline's time-in-the-brief segment is drawn; addendum (text, timestamp); turn_delivered (turn id); turn_response_locked (hold, revise, or reverse, justification, confidence); defense_question (question id, selecting event id); defense_answer (text, duration); draft_band (dimension, band or unassessed, evidence event ids); band_decision (dimension, confirmed or overridden, band, note); claim_neutralized (claim id, reason); run_voided (reason); debrief_opened; debrief_answer (which stance they would change, what they will do differently).

Claim table, one row per consequential claim in the variant: claim id and version, evidence status, failure family, importance, consequence level, warranted stance, stance taken and when, actions run, relied on or not, neutralized or not. Confidence at frame, lock, and after the Turn, the per-run points under the displayed mapping, and the False Challenge Rate (with all consequential claims in the run as its denominator) are computed from the trace and included in the export.

Nothing in the trace is inferred: every field is an action the student took, a value the student typed, a decision the faculty seat made, an authored attribute of the package, or a draft band the model produced against the Appendix A descriptors, which carries the evidence events and the quoted text it was read from (7.13).

The trace has two forms. The course export above carries the run weight, the mapping, and the points, because the instructor enters them in the gradebook of record (7.19). The copy held in the Judgment Record carries the events and the claim table without those three fields, because the record holds bands and never the points computed from them (7.13, 7.16).

### The strongest objection, and the answer

The objection: Tassl uses AI to decide whether students relied on AI correctly and asks everyone to trust its judgment about judgment. If the assistant inside the scenario can be confidently wrong, so can the system scoring the student, and Tassl has built a product whose central claim is that machine outputs should not be taken on faith. Under the AI-assisted authoring path (7.18) the objection sharpens: the standard the student is measured against was itself drafted by a model.

Tassl never judges judgment in the abstract, and the architecture exists so that it does not have to. Every consequential assessment runs against a standard a human expert fixed in advance: written by hand, or generated by AI from a published case and confirmed element by element by a disciplinary authority before any student saw it (7.18). A defect is a defect because that authority declared it one and stated why. A decision is defensible because it falls inside an answer space that authority confirmed before any student saw the scenario. A Turn warrants revision or does not because the authority decided and, once a scenario has been through field calibration, because a calibration cohort confirmed the scenario behaves as intended; the build scenario has the confirmation and not yet the cohort, and until the pilot the replay and debrief label that judgment as uncalibrated. Tassl observes what the student did against that authored standard and presents the comparison as plotted traces with the evidence attached, the way a flight simulator presents a session to the instructor: closer to reading an instrument record against a human-written standard than to forming an opinion about a person.

Where genuine interpretation is required, at band boundaries and in reading a defense, Tassl drafts from the graphs and a human with the disciplinary expertise decides from the same graphs; the draft boundaries themselves are in Appendix A, pending the builder's edit and pilot calibration. That instructor's judgment is final, they may override without giving a justification, and Tassl monitors its own override rate precisely because a rate near zero would mean the human check had become a formality. The grade a student receives is the instructor's mapping of bands that instructor confirmed (7.19); Tassl computes the arithmetic and pronounces nothing about the person. In the pilot, a sampled share of runs, initially proposed at 10 percent monthly and set as a pilot parameter (Section 8), is re-scored by hand on identified records under the written data agreement to catch drift, and a student who disagrees can appeal within 7 days of confirmation and gets a person's decision with a reason, with the time to that decision a pilot parameter alongside the appeal window (Section 8, Limits, quotas, and escalation).

The objection also cuts the other way. The current alternative is not a reliable human assessment Tassl would be replacing. It is a professor reading a polished document they no longer trust, forming an impression they cannot evidence, and assigning a grade a capable assistant could have earned for the student. Tassl replaces an unfalsifiable impression with a claim-by-claim record a student can read, argue with, and appeal. It is not asking anyone to trust a machine's opinion, but to look at what the student did.

## Appendix A: Draft Band Boundaries (for your edit)

### A.0 Status and how to read this appendix

This appendix holds the draft descriptors for the four bands of each of the seven dimensions in 7.13 (Novice, Developing, Proficient, Professional) and the three interior boundaries between them. The descriptors were drafted only from placements this document already fixes: the rules and edge cases of 7.1 to 7.21, the principles in Section 5, the Section 6 journey, the roles and lifecycle table in Section 8, the information items in Section 9, the metrics in Section 10, the risks in Section 11, and the walkthrough in Section 12. Nothing here adds a scoring rule. Where the PRD fixes no placement, the cell says so.

Who does what, in order. The editor drafted every cell and every boundary sentence below. The builder edits the three interior boundary sentences of each dimension, each prefixed [EDIT], before the build; the Novice and Professional descriptors and the fixed modifiers restate PRD text and change only if that text changes. The build scores against the edited draft, uncalibrated. In the walkthrough, the person in the faculty seat reads each drafted band and its evidence against these descriptors and confirms or changes it, which is the review 7.13 and 7.17 describe. The pilot calibrates the boundaries against real cohorts through inter-rater agreement, override patterns, and subgroup differences (7.13). Until then a boundary is a descriptive draft against the authored standard, not a validated cut score.

How to read the Anchor column.

- "Fixed: 7.x" means the PRD names the band or an extreme for this behavior (bottom, top, lowest band, high, mid-band, Professional, Novice).
- "Direction from 7.x; band proposed, not anchored" means the PRD describes the behavior and says which way it scores but does not say which band it lands in. The band placement is the editor's proposal.
- "Proposed, not anchored" means no PRD passage places the behavior. The descriptor is the editor's proposal, written to sit between its neighbours.

Rules that apply to every dimension, restated from the PRD.

- A band is a descriptive placement against the authored standard (7.13, 12), read from the run's recorded traces: reading order, delegation log, stance record, interrogation history, clock, confidence at three points, Turn response, and defense (7.2, 7.6, 7.7, 7.8, 7.11, 7.12, 9). No composite score, rank, or trait claim is produced (7.13).
- Tassl drafts the band with its evidence; the instructor confirms or changes it, and that decision is final (6, 7.13, 7.17). Runs close enough to a boundary that instructor judgment would change the result go to the review queue ahead of the batch (7.17).
- A dimension that cannot be assessed is reported as unassessed, never estimated (Section 7 standing rules). A run that loses more than a third of its consequential claims' stance records cannot be scored and is voided and re-offered at no cost, with no points recorded; a smaller loss leaves the run standing with Calibration and Verification reported unassessed (7.8, 7.13). Defense Missed caps Ownership only; other dimensions stay intact (7.12).
- A correction for Tassl's own error can raise a band or neutralize a dimension, never lower a band or the points computed from it (Section 7 standing rules; lifecycle table, Adjusted). Neutralization applies to affected claims (Section 7), to Adaptation on a miscalibrated Turn (7.11), and to Verification and Calibration on a retired variant (7.21).
- Communication quality is separated from domain reasoning and reliance behavior (7.13). The brief is never scored on writing, structure, or tone (7.10). Fluency, accent, grammar, and pace are excluded from the defense rubric, and a second-language defense is scored on content alone (7.12).
- Readiness results give context for interpreting escalation and verification and never justify a harsher band for a knowledge gap (7.1). The scored core is common across Guided, Standard, and Open, so a mode change is not a band change; mode is recorded beside the band (7.1, 7.16).
- Critique Runs are banded on Verification, Calibration, and Ownership only (7.15).
- Novice across every dimension raises an instructor flag and a recommendation to revisit foundations (7.13, 7.14, 7.17). Professional across all seven surfaces the harder variant next (7.14).
- Where a course maps bands to points (7.19), an edit to a boundary changes the points a run earns; the mapping is set in 7.19, not here.
- "Band" in this appendix means one of the four scoring bands. The "escalation band" in 7.1 is a Readiness Check outcome, not a scoring band.

### A.1 Framing

Framing concerns the decision and load-bearing assumptions (7.13). The frame holds the real decision in at most 50 words, three load-bearing assumptions at 25 words each, an initial position of 100 words that may be a lean, and a confidence number from 0 to 100, locked before the assistant unlocks (7.4).

Fixed modifiers. An assumption named in the frame that the Turn later disrupts is credited in Adaptation, not here (7.4). - [EDIT] Developing to Proficient: A frame reaches Proficient when the position sits inside the answer space and at least two of the three assumptions are load-bearing and testable against Evidence Room material.

Fixed modifiers. A Coherence Gap is a coaching hypothesis, not evidence of misconduct, and carries no automatic penalty (7.12). A defense taken at hour 47 after reconstructing the reasoning is legitimate and recorded as timing (7.12). A poorly scored decision defended well moves Decision Quality up (7.12). One word per field in the frame and undeclared outside use cost here, from the student's own record, or not at all (7.4, 7.6). Nothing answered is separated in the debrief into not knowing the content and not knowing one's own work (7.12). In Critique Runs, Ownership is banded from two follow-up questions, spoken or typed, 90 seconds total (7.15).

- [EDIT] Novice to Developing: Ownership leaves Novice when the student attempts the questions and can state, in their own words, what they decided and what the assistant was asked to do.
- [EDIT] Developing to Proficient: Ownership reaches Proficient when provenance questions (which document, which cohort, from when) are answered correctly for the claims the decision rested on, and any gap is admitted with a stated next step rather than guessed.
- [EDIT] Proficient to Professional: Ownership reaches Professional when every follow-up the run asked was answered without reference to the brief, or no follow-up was asked because every first answer already named its source, number, or reason (7.12); when the assistant's role and its unchecked claims are described accurately; and when the frame-to-revision reasoning holds under the counterfactual question the confirmed bank carries for the scenario, of the "what payback becomes if churn is 20 percent worse" kind (6). Where the bank carries no counterfactual question, the condition is not applied and the band rests on the first two.

### A.8 What the PRD does not fix

- Proficient is unanchored in all seven dimensions. Every Proficient descriptor and both boundaries around it are proposals.
- Developing is fixed only in Calibration (the Section 6 appeal) and, by the reading of "mid-band" above, for the holding component of Adaptation (7.11).
- Professional is fixed in Verification (6) and Calibration (7.8, 7.9, 7.13, 6, 10), and by direction in Delegation (7.5) and Adaptation (7.4, 7.11). Framing, Decision Quality, and Ownership have no Professional placement.
- Novice is fixed in Calibration (7.8, 7.15, 6), Decision Quality (7.10), Ownership (7.12, lifecycle table), and Adaptation (7.11: an implicit hold where the Turn warranted a change is bottom, as is the missing justification), and by direction in Framing (7.4), Delegation (7.5, 7.2), and Verification (7.2, 7.8).
- The PRD fixes no band for the confidence line by itself (7.8, 7.13).
- Two fixed placements sit in tension with a rule in the same section, and the builder may want to resolve them while editing: 7.8 places accepting everything at the top of Calibration in a defect-free run, while the 7.8 rule above it says a sound claim may still warrant verification when it is load-bearing, cheap to check, or weakly sourced; 7.7 scores zero actions well in a defect-free run on the same reading. The descriptors above follow the fixed placements. This is a decision the builder has to make before the build, because the sound-variant step of the walkthrough (Section 12) turns on whether a sound claim that is load-bearing or cheap to check carries a warranted stance of Accept or of Verify.
- No numeric threshold in this appendix other than the 15 percent False Challenge Rate reference band (itself a hypothesis, 10) comes from the PRD. Any number the builder adds while editing should be labeled a hypothesis, consistent with the rest of the document.
